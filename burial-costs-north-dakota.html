Burial Costs in North Dakota (2026)

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Last updated: October 29, 2026 Last reviewed: October 30, 2026 What changed: North Dakota burial guide reworked around the burial permit, human service zone help and veterans cemeteries, and unsourced savings claims dropped. Region: Midwest

A North Dakota burial arrives as three bills: about $6,700 for the funeral home's services, plot and interment charges at the cemetery that start near $3,000, and a headstone or marker at $500–$5,000. The state also sets a deadline. Under N.D.C.C. § 23-06-04, the body must be disposed of within eight days of death unless an exception applies, such as a longer-hold permit from the local health officer. Before buying a plot, check the deed history too: § 23-06-21.1 lets a cemetery start proceedings to take back a deeded space that has gone unused for burial for more than sixty years.

Spending Less on a North Dakota Burial

Ways to keep a North Dakota burial within budget:

  • Ask the cemetery for its own prices. The FTC Funeral Rule does not cover cemeteries, so request the plot, opening, liner and marker charges in writing.
  • Know who runs the cemetery. Church, fraternal, charitable and city cemeteries are exempt from the state's perpetual care licensing, so their prices and rules come from the congregation, lodge or city; ask them directly.
  • Compare at least two funeral homes. Each must give you a General Price List, and the direct burial line on it shows the lowest-cost burial that home sells.
  • Choose a direct burial. Skipping the viewing and embalming removes those charges, and the family can hold a memorial later. It is the lowest burial price on each General Price List.

Comparing North Dakota Funeral Homes

Calling two or three North Dakota funeral homes and going through their General Price Lists line by line is a quick way to see where they differ. The FTC Funeral Rule requires them to give prices over the phone, and the State Board of Funeral Service (701-776-6222) takes complaints if one will not.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a typical North Dakota burial price?

By our numbers, $14,910 to $19,410: a $6,700 funeral service, a $3,000 plot, a $2,010 casket, a $1,200 liner or vault, $1,500 to open and close the grave, and a marker priced from $500 to $5,000.

Eligible veterans can be buried at the North Dakota Veterans Cemetery near Mandan, where the grave, opening and closing and the government marker cost the family nothing, which removes most of the cemetery side of the bill. The State Board of Funeral Service (701-776-6222) licenses funeral homes.

Does North Dakota law require a burial vault?

No North Dakota statute calls for a vault, though the cemetery may. We estimate the container at about $1,200, and a plain concrete liner is normally the cheapest one a cemetery takes. Get the cemetery's container rules in writing before you buy one, since a vault bought elsewhere still has to meet them.

Perpetual care cemeteries in North Dakota are licensed through the county recorder on state health department forms and renew each year by July 1 (NDCC chapter 23-21.1). Church, fraternal, charitable and city cemeteries are exempt, so a rule at one of those is set entirely by the congregation, lodge or city.

How can a North Dakota family pay less for burial?

A direct burial skips embalming and the viewing and is normally the cheapest burial a funeral home sells. A casket bought elsewhere must be accepted without a handling fee (FTC Funeral Rule), and an eligible veteran's grave at Fargo National Cemetery or the state veterans cemetery is free.

Who sets cemetery prices in North Dakota?

Each cemetery sets its own prices. Perpetual care cemeteries are licensed through the county recorder, while church, fraternal, charitable and city cemeteries are exempt and set their own (NDCC chapter 23-21.1), so ask each for its price list.

What is the complete North Dakota burial cost?

Totalled, the North Dakota parts come to $14,910 to $19,410 before cash advance items such as death certificates and an obituary. The funeral home bills the $6,700 service and, if you buy it there, the $2,010 casket; the cemetery bills the $3,000 plot, the $1,200 liner and the $1,500 opening. The marker can come from the cemetery or a monument dealer.

Is natural burial an option in North Dakota?

Policies differ from cemetery to cemetery, so ask each whether it takes an unembalmed burial without a vault in a biodegradable container. The questions to put to a cemetery are in our green burial guide.

North Dakota's disinterment permit and the receiving-vault exception

Moving a buried body in North Dakota takes a disinterment permit from the state registrar. The application must be signed by the next of kin and by the licensed embalmer in charge of the disinterment, unless a court has ordered the move. One permit covers digging up the remains, transporting them and burying them again. Where a whole group of graves must be moved, one permit can cover all of them, as long as each body is identified as far as possible and the old and new locations are listed (N.D. Admin. Code § 33-04-10-04; checked October 29, 2026).

Winter deaths are common in a state where the ground freezes hard. A body that an embalmer has prepared and placed in a receiving vault is not treated as disinterred when it comes out for burial in spring, so no permit is needed for that step. The original burial-transit permit is filed with the county recorder, who keeps it permanently.

North Dakota burial oversight and public help

The North Dakota State Board of Funeral Service (701-776-6222) licenses North Dakota funeral homes and takes complaints about them. Perpetual care cemeteries are licensed through the county recorder, so ask the cemetery who oversees it.

North Dakota is not one of the nine states that the Funeral Consumers Alliance counts as requiring a funeral director, so a family can arrange a burial itself once it has the permit, within the cemetery's own rules.

If plans change to cremation, North Dakota has no waiting period, but a disposition-transit permit and the next of kin's signed authorization are required (N.D.C.C. § 23-06-07).

Human service zone help

When there is not enough money and no responsible relative, the human service zone arranges the disposition and pays $3,500; it is a cremation by default, and a burial is approved instead when religious beliefs require it (N.D.C.C. § 23-06-03(6)). An eligible spouse or child can also claim Social Security's one-time $255, which must be requested within two years and goes to the survivor rather than the cemetery.

Veterans

Fargo National Cemetery (701-451-4650), the North Dakota Veterans Cemetery near Mandan (701-667-1418) and the tribal All Nations Veterans Cemetery at Fort Yates (701-854-8689) bury eligible veterans at no charge for the grave, opening, liner or headstone, as listed by the VA National Cemetery Administration.

North Dakota burial steps

  1. Get prices from two cemeteries and two funeral homes; the plot, opening and marker are priced apart from the service.
  2. Ask the cemetery about its winter rules and receiving vault.
  3. If the family needs help, and burial is a religious requirement, tell the human service zone before buying a plot.
  4. For a veteran, have the DD214 ready when you call Fargo or Mandan.
  5. Ask whether each funeral home's direct burial price includes the burial permit and the trip to the cemetery.
  6. Complaints go to the Board at 701-776-6222.

Burial Costs in Oklahoma (2026)

Reviewed: February 2026 Region: South

Oklahoma's burial traditions are uniquely shaped by the state's Native American heritage, oil-industry history, and strong community bonds that define life across the Great Plains. With 39 federally recognized tribal nations, Oklahoma has more tribal cemeteries and culturally distinct burial practices than almost any other state. Affordable costs make traditional burial accessible for most families, with Oklahoma City and Tulsa as the primary markets and vast rural areas offering even lower prices. From the historic Fort Gibson National Cemetery to small-town church graveyards, this guide covers what Oklahoma families need to know about burial costs and options.

What Drives Burial Costs in Oklahoma

Oklahoma ranks among the most affordable states for burial, thanks to low land costs, a competitive funeral market, and a strong tradition of community-based services. Oklahoma City and Tulsa — the state's two major metros — have a mix of corporate and independent funeral providers, keeping prices competitive. Rose Hill Burial Park and Memorial Park Cemetery are among OKC's largest, while Memorial Park in Tulsa serves the northeastern part of the state.

The state's Native American heritage profoundly influences the burial landscape. Oklahoma's 39 tribal nations maintain their own cemeteries and burial customs — from Cherokee to Chickasaw, Choctaw to Comanche — each with specific traditions regarding preparation, burial orientation, and ceremonial requirements. Tribal cemeteries generally operate independently of state regulation and are available to tribal members.

Fort Gibson National Cemetery, established in 1868, is one of the most historic national cemeteries in the country. Along with Fort Sill National Cemetery, it provides free burial for eligible Oklahoma veterans. Oklahoma's oil industry history has also left its mark, with elaborate monuments and family mausoleum plots in some Tulsa-area cemeteries reflecting the state's oil-boom wealth of the early 20th century.

Cemetery Types Available in Oklahoma

  • Municipal cemeteries — Oklahoma City, Tulsa, Norman, and other cities operate public cemeteries with regulated pricing. Rose Hill Burial Park in OKC and Memorial Park in Tulsa are the largest.
  • Tribal cemeteries — Oklahoma's 39 tribal nations maintain dedicated burial grounds following their specific cultural traditions. These are generally available only to enrolled tribal members and their families.
  • Church cemeteries — Baptist, Methodist, and Catholic church cemeteries are common throughout Oklahoma, particularly in rural areas. Many offer affordable burial to members and sometimes to the broader community.
  • National cemeteries — Fort Gibson National Cemetery and Fort Sill National Cemetery provide free burial for eligible veterans with full military honors.
  • Rural community cemeteries — Small-town cemeteries across Oklahoma offer some of the most affordable plots in the state, often under $500.
  • Private memorial parks — Modern memorial parks in the OKC and Tulsa metros offer mausoleum entombment, cremation gardens, and premium sections.

Casket and Vault Options

Oklahoma funeral homes average $1,830 for caskets — well below the national average. Online retailers offer additional savings, with comparable caskets for $500-$1,500. The FTC Funeral Rule ensures that Oklahoma funeral homes must accept third-party caskets without handling fees. Native American burial traditions vary by tribe — some prefer simple wooden caskets, while others have specific cultural requirements for burial containers.

Most Oklahoma cemeteries require a burial vault or grave liner, though this is cemetery policy rather than state law. The state's red clay soil can cause significant ground settling without a vault. Concrete liners start around $800. Some rural and tribal cemeteries do not require vaults.

Consumer Rights for Burial in Oklahoma

  • FTC Funeral Rule — All Oklahoma funeral homes must provide itemized General Price Lists and cannot require package purchases.
  • Oklahoma Funeral Board — Regulates funeral directors and establishments, handling consumer complaints.
  • Embalming not required — Oklahoma does not mandate embalming by law. Families may decline embalming for direct burial or when refrigeration is available.
  • Casket purchase freedom — Third-party caskets must be accepted without handling fees.
  • Home burial permitted — Oklahoma allows burial on private property with proper permits and compliance with local regulations.
  • Preneed protections — Oklahoma law governs prepaid funeral contracts with consumer protections for trust funds.

Green and Natural Burial in Oklahoma

Green burial options are emerging in Oklahoma, though certified natural burial grounds are limited. Some rural cemeteries accommodate natural burial practices — biodegradable caskets, no embalming, and no vaults — as a matter of tradition rather than formal green certification. Oklahoma's home burial laws provide another pathway for families seeking natural burial on private land. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does burial cost in Oklahoma?

A traditional burial in Oklahoma costs approximately $6,100 for the funeral service plus $2,700 for a cemetery plot. Total costs range from $11,230 to $15,760. Oklahoma is one of the most affordable states for burial, with rural areas offering total costs under $8,000.

Is a burial vault required in Oklahoma?

Oklahoma has no state law requiring burial vaults. Most municipal and memorial park cemeteries require them as policy to prevent ground settling in the state's clay soil. Some rural and tribal cemeteries do not require vaults.

What are the cheapest burial options in Oklahoma?

Direct burial in a rural community cemetery with an online casket can cost under $5,000 total. Some small-town cemetery plots cost under $500. Veterans receive free burial at Fort Gibson or Fort Sill National Cemeteries.

Are there burial options for Native Americans in Oklahoma?

Yes. Oklahoma's 39 tribal nations maintain their own cemeteries with culturally specific burial practices. Tribal members should work with their tribal government for burial arrangements. Some tribes also assist with funeral costs for enrolled members through tribal burial assistance programs.

What is Fort Gibson National Cemetery?

Fort Gibson National Cemetery, established in 1868 in eastern Oklahoma, is one of the most historic national cemeteries in the country. It provides free burial for eligible veterans and their spouses, including a plot, vault, opening/closing, and government headstone. It is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

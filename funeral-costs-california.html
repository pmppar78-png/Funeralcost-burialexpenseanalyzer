Funeral Costs in California: What Families Pay in 2026

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, FTC Funeral Rule Region: West

California has the country's priciest funeral quintile, a 65% cremation rate, and the most unusual disposition law in the US — alkaline hydrolysis since 2017, human composting rolling out in 2027. This guide walks through what families actually pay, the state-specific rules that shape the bill, and the handful of large operators who quietly set the pricing floor from Sacramento to San Diego.

San Francisco hasn't allowed new burials since 1902

A 1902 city ordinance banned new burials within San Francisco city limits, and a 1912 follow-up forced most existing cemeteries to exhume and relocate remains. The entire town of Colma — a few miles south — exists because of it. Today Colma has roughly 1,500 living residents and an estimated 1.5 million dead, including Wyatt Earp, Joe DiMaggio, and Levi Strauss. For San Francisco families, this means your "local" burial is almost certainly a Colma burial: Cypress Lawn (founded 1892), Holy Cross, Olivet, or one of the smaller denominational grounds. The SF Medical Examiner's office runs an indigent disposition program for residents who die with no next-of-kin and no means.

Practically, it adds a transportation line item — a San Francisco funeral home will quote a hearse trip down 280 or 101, and ceremony staff hours run whether the service is graveside in Colma or back in the city.

The state with the strangest disposition menu in America

California legalized alkaline hydrolysis — "water cremation" or aquamation — in 2017 under AB 967. A handful of providers in Los Angeles, San Diego, and the Bay Area now offer it, generally priced between standard direct cremation and low-end full cremation. And AB 351, signed in 2022, made California the fifth state to authorize natural organic reduction (human composting); licensed facilities are expected to come online in 2027, with pilot providers already accepting pre-need arrangements.

  • Flame cremation — legal statewide, 48-hour waiting period before the cremation itself, authorization required from an FD or next-of-kin.
  • Alkaline hydrolysis (AB 967, 2017) — legal; limited availability; typically $2,500–$4,500 for direct service.
  • Natural organic reduction (AB 351, 2022) — operational in 2027; early pre-need quotes around $7,000.
  • Green/conservation burial — Fernwood (Mill Valley), Forever Fernwood, Joshua Tree Memorial Park, and Purissima Cemetery (Half Moon Bay) are the main certified sites.
  • Burial at sea — permitted by EPA Region 9 at least 3 nautical miles offshore with notification.

The operators who quietly set California prices

Most families never realize that a handful of organizations set the price floor for the entire state. Forest Lawn alone operates six cemetery-mortuary complexes — Glendale, Hollywood Hills, Covina Hills, Cypress, Long Beach, and Cathedral City — and maintains its own General Price List across all of them. Rose Hills in Whittier is the largest cemetery in North America by annual interments. Cypress Lawn in Colma holds much of San Francisco's historic burial trade. And the Neptune Society runs the largest cremation-focused chain in the state, with storefront operations designed to undercut traditional funeral home pricing.

Riverside National and California's veteran burial capacity

California is home to Riverside National Cemetery — the largest of the VA's 155 national cemeteries by annual interments, with more than 250,000 graves and room to continue operating for decades. For an eligible veteran and their spouse, interment at Riverside is fully covered by VA benefits: the plot, opening and closing, the government headstone, a vault liner, and perpetual care. California also operates state veterans cemeteries at Yountville (Napa Valley) and Igo (Shasta County), with a Seaside site near Monterey serving central-coast families.

California-specific checklist: Request the GPL in writing from every provider (required by the Funeral Bureau, not just the FTC). For pre-need, confirm the 30% master trust requirement has been met. For cremation, expect the mandatory 48-hour hold from time of death before cremation can occur. Use our comparison worksheet to keep the quotes straight.

Two regulators, one invoice

California splits funeral consumer protection between the FTC (Funeral Rule) and the state's Cemetery and Funeral Bureau, a division of the Department of Consumer Affairs. The state layer matters: California requires funeral establishments to file and maintain their GPLs with the Bureau, imposes a 30% trust requirement on pre-need funeral contracts, and mandates a 48-hour waiting period between death and cremation except under specific medical exceptions. Cemetery operations have their own licensure track. Complaints at the state level go to the Bureau; federal complaints go to the FTC. Either can trigger an audit.

Your Consumer Rights in California

Know Your Rights Under the FTC Funeral Rule Every funeral home in California must comply with the Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule, which protects consumers nationwide. You have the right to: receive an itemized General Price List before making any decisions; choose only the services and products you want; purchase a casket or urn from a third party without penalty; decline embalming unless required by state law for specific circumstances; and receive a written estimate before services are performed.

If you believe a funeral home in California has violated these rights, you can file a complaint with the FTC at ftc.gov, the California Cemetery and Funeral Bureau, or the Funeral Consumers Alliance. The California AG's consumer protection division also fields complaints.

What to Do When Planning a Funeral in California

When an immediate California funeral arrangement lands on you, the order of operations matters. This sequence tends to save both money and emotional bandwidth:

  1. Take a breath. Unless there are legal or medical time constraints, you typically have 24–72 hours before decisions must be finalized.
  2. Request General Price Lists from at least 2–3 funeral homes in your area of California. They are legally required to provide them. Questions to ask funeral homes
  3. Decide on disposition: cremation in California or burial in California. This is the single biggest cost decision.
  4. Choose only the services you need. Embalming, premium caskets, and elaborate arrangements are optional. What funeral homes don't tell you
  5. Explore payment assistance if cost is a concern: Social Security benefits, veteran benefits, Medicaid assistance, or crowdfunding.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average price of a funeral in California right now?

Current averages in California come in at $10,600 for a traditional funeral, $7,800 for cremation with a service, and from $2,200 for direct cremation — with the traditional figure sitting 35% above the $7,848 US median. Cemetery and burial fees add roughly $5,200 when applicable. Because California pricing is not standardized, the same service can swing several thousand dollars between neighboring funeral homes, so always ask for the itemized GPL.

Is cremation cheaper than burial in California?

Yes — significantly. In California, direct cremation at $2,200 saves families roughly $8,400 compared with the $10,600 traditional funeral plus cemetery costs. The California cremation rate currently sits at 65%, above the national average of about 60%. Western states tend to have higher cremation rates, and the availability of green burial and alternative disposition options is growing across the region.

What rights do California families have when arranging a funeral?

Under the FTC Funeral Rule, every California funeral home is required to give you an itemized price list, allow you to decline any service you do not want, accept caskets or urns brought in from outside providers without surcharges, and avoid false claims about what the law requires. Check with the California funeral regulatory board for any additional state-level protections that apply locally.

How do I keep funeral costs down in California?

Three moves cut the most: compare at least three California General Price Lists side by side before choosing a provider, strip packages down to only the services you actually want, and choose direct cremation ($2,200) if cost is the priority. California has a mature green burial and direct cremation market; providers are accustomed to non-traditional arrangements and often price transparently. Conservation burial grounds and hybrid cemetery sections in California typically cost 30-50% less than traditional plots in the same cemetery. Then confirm eligibility for veteran, Medicaid, and Social Security survivor benefits.

Does California require embalming?

California law does not require embalming in most situations. It is generally a choice, not a legal obligation — though a funeral home may insist on it as an internal policy for certain open-casket viewings. Refrigeration is almost always a valid substitute. Western states generally have progressive disposition laws, and many communities offer a wider range of alternatives to traditional embalming and burial. Under the FTC Funeral Rule, no provider may claim embalming is legally required without pointing to a specific statute.

Is California cheaper or more expensive than the US average for funerals?

California lands 35% above the US median of $7,848 for a traditional funeral, with the state average at $10,600. Costs here are above the national average in most metro areas, though rural communities may see lower prices and the West region trends higher than the country overall. Which funeral option costs the least in California?

Direct cremation carries the lowest price tag in California, from roughly $2,200. You skip the viewing, ceremony, and embalming — transport, cremation, and return of the ashes are all that are included. Direct burial is the next step up at about $6,360 and is still considerably cheaper than a traditional service.

How can I find affordable funeral homes in California?

Ask three or more California providers for their General Price Lists and compare line by line; call the local Funeral Consumers Alliance chapter for vetted recommendations; look specifically for direct cremation specialists, which often undercut full-service providers; request the simplest or "basic services only" package; and check for nonprofit and cooperative funeral homes in your part of California.

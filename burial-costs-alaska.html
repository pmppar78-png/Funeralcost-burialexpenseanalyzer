Burial Costs in Alaska (2026)

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 Region: West

Burial in Alaska is logistically distinct in ways that don't apply in the Lower 48. Frozen ground governs the winter schedule, supply chains cross water or air, and most of the population lives in a handful of boroughs. Those realities show up on the invoice. What follows is the real range a family in Anchorage, Fairbanks, Juneau, or the Mat-Su Valley should expect in 2026 — and what drives a burial toward the top or bottom of that range.

Once every line is on the page, a full-service Alaska burial typically lands between $18,350 and $25,790. Direct burial, VA eligibility, and village-based burial traditions can put the number well under half of that.

Permafrost, frost depth, and why winter burials get scheduled

In Interior Alaska and anywhere north of the Alaska Range, the ground freezes to roughly four to six feet through the deep winter, and opening a grave requires either a thaw or specialized equipment. Most Fairbanks-area cemeteries, including Birch Hill Cemetery, schedule winter burials into the spring thaw window while holding remains in refrigeration or a receiving vault. This is routine — not a red flag — but families aren't always told upfront. Opening-and-closing invoices in winter can include a ground-thawing surcharge of several hundred dollars where equipment is available. In Anchorage and Juneau, where the maritime climate limits frost depth, winter burial is less disrupted but still slower than summer.

Shipping, remains transport, and the bush Alaska premium

Alaska has roughly 200 communities unreachable by road. When a death occurs in a village and the family wants burial in that village, remains move by scheduled air carrier — Ravn, Grant, Alaska Seaplanes — and the freight charges can run $800-$2,500 depending on distance and whether the destination accepts a basic transport casket or requires an air tray. Conversely, when a villager dies in Anchorage and is being returned home, the same charge applies in reverse. Experienced Alaska funeral homes build this into early estimates; newer arrivals sometimes don't. Ask specifically about transport whenever geography is involved.

Real Alaska cemeteries and what they charge for

  • Anchorage Memorial Park Cemetery — Anchorage's primary municipal cemetery since 1915, on Cordova Street. Traditional ground burial, columbarium, and a veterans' section. Pricing published by the municipality.
  • Angelus Memorial Park — Private memorial park on East 100th Avenue in Anchorage, generally pricing above the municipal cemetery.
  • Birch Hill Cemetery — Fairbanks-area cemetery operated by the Fairbanks North Star Borough; winter scheduling applies.
  • Fort Richardson National Cemetery — VA National Cemetery Administration site in Anchorage serving all eligible Alaska veterans under 38 U.S.C. § 2402; plot, vault, marker, and opening-and-closing are all covered, and a partial burial allowance is payable under 38 CFR § 3.1600.
  • Evergreen Cemetery — Juneau's historic cemetery, with Alaska-Yukon pioneer markers dating to the 1880s.
Casket economics in Alaska: Online casket delivery to Alaska is real but slower than the Lower 48 — plan 5-10 days for freight to Anchorage and longer to Fairbanks. The delivered price often still beats an Alaska funeral home's showroom casket by 30-50%. Titan Casket and Costco both ship to Alaska. The FTC Funeral Rule forbids the funeral home from charging a handling fee.

Benefits and assistance specific to Alaska

The state's regulator is the Board of Funeral Services under the Division of Corporations, Business and Professional Licensing. Complaints about price disclosure, unlicensed practice, or mishandling of remains route there. Separate from state oversight, Alaska Native regional corporations and tribal councils sometimes offer cultural or financial assistance for members' funerals — this is not widely advertised and is worth asking about directly through the regional corporation. The Alaska Department of Revenue's Permanent Fund Dividend can be assigned to a funeral home in certain hardship cases. Eligible veterans should start with the VA before signing anything private; Fort Richardson National Cemetery is often the lowest-cost path for a dignified burial in the state.

What drives an Alaska burial over $25,000

  • Air transport of remains from a village and back, or to the Lower 48
  • Winter opening-and-closing surcharges on frozen ground
  • Imported stone monuments shipped via barge or freight
  • Premium caskets purchased through the funeral home rather than online
  • Extended viewing or visitation periods at a full-service Anchorage or Fairbanks provider

Commonly Asked Questions

What do Alaska families spend on burial?

A traditional Alaska burial bills out to roughly $18,350-$25,790 all-in. That's $9,800 for the funeral service, $4,500 for a cemetery plot, $2,940 for a casket, $1,800 for a vault, $2,250 for opening-and-closing, and $1,000-$3,000 for a monument. If the death involves village transport, add $800-$2,500 in air freight. If the burial is scheduled in the Interior during winter, expect a ground-thaw surcharge. Families eligible for Fort Richardson National Cemetery see substantially lower out-of-pocket numbers.

Do I need a vault for burial in Alaska?

Alaska law does not mandate a vault, but cemetery-level requirements vary and the freeze-thaw cycle makes ground-settling a real issue here. Anchorage Memorial Park and most Mat-Su cemeteries require at least a liner; some traditional village cemeteries don't. Liners run $800-$1,500, full vaults $2,000-$10,000. Green and natural burial sections, where they exist, usually waive the requirement entirely. Get the cemetery's policy in writing before accepting the funeral home's default product.

What are the cheapest burial options in Alaska?

The genuinely cheapest paths are VA burial at Fort Richardson National Cemetery (free for eligible veterans and spouses), direct burial with no ceremony, and burial on traditional village or Native corporation land where that's available. Direct burial in Anchorage generally runs $3,500-$5,500 when combined with a municipal cemetery plot. Buying the casket online through Titan Casket or Costco cuts another $1,500-$3,000 off even a modest funeral home selection. Green burial sections exist in a few Alaska cemeteries and avoid the vault line item entirely.

How do cemetery prices differ across Alaska?

The Anchorage municipal cemetery generally prices below the private Anchorage memorial parks, which generally price below cemeteries on Kodiak or in the Southeast due to barge freight on stone and supplies. Fairbanks sits in its own category because winter scheduling and frost-depth work change the opening-and-closing math. Village cemeteries operate under tribal or regional corporation rules and often waive cash pricing entirely for members. Call two Anchorage options plus Fort Richardson if veteran benefits apply; the spread across those three is usually $10,000-$15,000.

Once I add every line item, what does burial actually cost in Alaska?

$9,800 funeral service + $4,500 plot + $2,940 casket + $1,800 vault + $2,250 opening-and-closing + $1,000-$3,000 headstone produces the $18,350-$25,790 range. Air transport, winter ground-thawing, and imported stone monuments can push the upper bound past $30,000 in specific cases. Document everything in writing up front — including the transport leg if it applies — because verbal estimates in Alaska miss freight more often than anywhere else.

Can Alaska families choose a natural or green burial?

Green burial exists in Alaska but in limited form. A few Anchorage-area providers and cemeteries accommodate natural burial with shrouds or biodegradable caskets and no embalming. Traditional village burial practices in several Alaska Native communities already align closely with what the green burial movement formalizes elsewhere. If you want a Green Burial Council-certified site specifically, the list is thin — but the functional option of a no-vault, no-embalming burial is available in more places than the marketing suggests. Ask cemeteries directly rather than relying on their website.

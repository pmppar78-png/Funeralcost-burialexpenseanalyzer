Burial Costs in Utah (2026)

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 Region: Mountain

Planning a burial in Utah is more involved than most people realize — there's the funeral home, the cemetery, the casket, the vault, the headstone, and the paperwork, and each comes with its own price list. We've broken down current Utah costs so you can compare providers honestly and avoid the charges that often get bundled quietly into a package.

Salt Lake City Cemetery: the largest city-owned in the country

Salt Lake City Cemetery — 250 acres above downtown — is the largest municipally owned cemetery in the United States. The city runs a historic-plot program that allows Salt Lake residents to receive free burial in designated sections that hold older, reclaimed graves, a program that has no direct analog in any other major US city. Non-historic plots are priced well below the state average as well. Mount Olivet near the University of Utah and Wasatch Lawn in Millcreek handle most of the private-cemetery volume; Utah Valley Memorial Park serves the Provo-Orem corridor. For a Salt Lake family, starting the cemetery conversation with the city clerk's office — not with a private park — routinely produces the single largest reduction available.

Utah's permissive home-burial statute

Utah has some of the country's more permissive family-burial and home-burial laws. With proper permits, deed-recorded cemetery designation, and compliance with local setback rules, rural families can legally bury on their own property outside municipal corporate limits. The practical obligations are real — the burial ground becomes a title burden in perpetuity, and county health-department notification is required — but for families with agricultural or ranch land the option eliminates plot, opening-and-closing, and perpetual-care charges entirely. The DOPL Funeral Service Board is the state licensing authority for funeral directors and fields questions about the paperwork. According to the Utah Board of Funeral Service (Division of Professional Licensing), all licensed establishments must comply with state price-disclosure and consumer-protection rules.

Winter elevation closures and the storage-fee line item

At elevation, Utah cemeteries routinely pause ground operations from roughly November through April and hold remains in a vault until spring thaw. Daggett, Duchesne, Summit, and Wasatch county cemeteries all enforce some form of seasonal closure. Vault storage fees vary widely — some include 30 days in the base opening-and-closing charge, others bill daily at $25–$50. Always ask whether opening-and-closing is priced at time of service or at spring interment, and whether storage is flat or metered. A Park City family choosing a January funeral often sees storage fees rival the plot cost itself.

FTC Funeral Rule (16 CFR Part 453) in Utah: Every licensed Utah mortuary must provide itemized General Price Lists, cannot charge a handling fee on third-party caskets, and must unbundle services on request. Cemeteries are governed separately and are not covered by the federal rule. Salt Lake, Mount Olivet, and Wasatch Lawn all will itemize if asked. Honest shopping tactics for Utah

  • Price the cemetery and funeral home as two separate bills. Bundled quotes obscure where the markup sits — and the markup sits in a different place depending on the provider.
  • Call the county clerk if burial is outside a metro. Sparse-population counties often hold fee schedules only in the clerk's office; plot fees can run 40–60% below Salt Lake private cemeteries.
  • Ask about Salt Lake's historic-plot program if the decedent or family is a city resident.
  • Confirm the winter window before committing to a cemetery and a date.
  • Third-party casket. Casket markup at Utah mortuaries commonly runs 300–400%.
  • Grave liner versus vault. Most Utah cemeteries accept a liner at roughly 40% of full-vault cost.
  • Independent monument work. The headstone cost guide covers local stonework pricing.

Green burial and the alternatives picture

Utah does not yet have a certified conservation burial ground, but several Wasatch Front cemeteries maintain hybrid green sections accepting shroud or biodegradable-container burials without vault or embalming. Availability changes quickly — call directly rather than relying on websites. The green burial options guide covers what to ask for. Utah's cremation rate hovers around 45%, lower than most neighboring states, and LDS cultural traditions continue to favor burial over cremation for the majority of residents, which keeps the state's burial cemeteries active and competitive.

Paying for burial in Utah

Utah does not operate a large state indigent-burial program; limited assistance exists through the Department of Workforce Services for qualifying cases. County welfare offices handle some direct-burial cases. Veterans should first check eligibility for Utah Veterans Memorial Park in Camp Williams (Bluffdale), which provides free interment for eligible veterans and qualifying dependents — full details in veteran burial benefits. The funeral payment assistance guide covers Social Security lump-sum and FEMA reimbursement; cheapest funeral options maps the floor of Utah arrangements, which can land below $3,500 with direct burial. Eligibility and benefits fall under the VA National Cemetery Administration (38 U.S.C. § 2402) and the VA burial allowance rules at 38 CFR § 3.1600.

What Readers Typically Ask

What is the full price of burial in Utah?

A full burial in Utah comes in at about $6,900 for the funeral service, $3,200 for the cemetery plot, $2,070 for a casket, $1,280 for a vault, $1,600 for opening and closing, and $1,000-$3,000 for a headstone. Totals generally land between $12,980 and $18,250 depending on choices.

Is a burial vault required in Utah?

Burial vaults are required by most cemeteries in Utah as a matter of cemetery policy (to prevent ground settling), though they are rarely mandated by state law. A grave liner — a less expensive alternative to a full vault — may also meet the cemetery's requirements. Always ask the specific cemetery about their policies and whether cheaper alternatives are accepted. Vault costs in Utah typically range from $800 to $10,000.

What is the best way to shop cemeteries in Utah?

Ask each Utah cemetery for a full itemized price sheet covering the plot, opening-and-closing fees, vault or liner requirements, perpetual-care charge, and any residency or denominational rules. Cemeteries are not covered by the FTC Funeral Rule, so they will only provide these details when asked directly. Notably, rural Utah cemeteries served by county clerks often price far below Salt Lake City-area private cemeteries, and seasonal ground-closing windows at elevation can affect the interment timeline.

Does Utah have green burial cemeteries?

There are green burial options in parts of Utah, though coverage is not statewide. Green burial removes embalming and the vault requirement and uses a biodegradable container, typically coming in below a traditional burial cost. Some Utah cemeteries have hybrid sections rather than fully dedicated grounds. Check our green burial guide and call local Utah cemeteries to confirm what they currently offer.

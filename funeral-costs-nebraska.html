Funeral Costs in Nebraska: What Families Pay in 2026

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, FTC Funeral Rule Region: Midwest

Nebraska is the rare Midwestern state where funeral pricing has stayed remarkably honest — in part because the same families own the same funeral homes for three and four generations. Heafey Hoffmann Dworak & Cutler has served Omaha since 1892. Braman Mortuary runs the north-central Nebraska circuit from Norfolk. Kremer Funeral Home has been in Aurora since 1885. A traditional funeral in Nebraska averages $6,700, 15% below the national figure reported in the NFDA 2024 General Price List Study, and direct cremation starts at $1,500. The state's 50% cremation rate is close to the national midpoint, but Omaha and Lincoln run higher while much of the Sandhills remains burial country — Catholic, Lutheran, and Methodist cemeteries still fill most of the space on the disposition map.

Four-Generation Funeral Homes and Nebraska's Quiet Pricing Stability

Nebraska has comparatively little of the corporate-chain rollup that transformed funeral pricing in Texas, Florida, and Arizona. Service Corporation International and Carriage Services operate a handful of Nebraska locations, but the majority of the state's 250-plus funeral establishments remain family-owned — often by the same family for a century or more. Heafey-Heafey-Hoffmann-Dworak-Cutler is in its fifth generation. Roeder Mortuary and Crosby-Kunold-Burket trace back similar distances. When the same family runs the business and their kids go to the same schools as their clients' kids, non-declinable service fees tend to stay disciplined — typically $1,800-$2,400 in Nebraska compared to $2,800+ in heavily consolidated markets.

This matters at the GPL level. Two Omaha funeral homes a mile apart will quote within $400-$700 of each other for the same package. The larger variance appears at the rural/metro boundary: Lincoln and Omaha GPLs run $400-$800 higher than comparable Hastings, Kearney, or Grand Island quotes. Nebraska's 50% cremation rate has been climbing roughly a point a year, and a handful of Omaha and Lincoln direct-cremation specialists (Cremation Society of Nebraska, Cremation Plus) have published flat-rate $995-$1,295 offerings that pull down the state direct-cremation average.

Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services and the Funeral Licensing Board

Nebraska licenses funeral directors, embalmers, and funeral establishments through the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services Funeral Directing and Embalming Board, authorized by Nebraska Revised Statutes 38-1401 through 38-1434. The FTC Funeral Rule (16 CFR Part 453) governs pricing disclosures in parallel with the state board's licensing authority. Nebraska requires 100% of preneed funeral trust principal be placed in trust, and the buyer retains a right to cancel with full refund of principal plus a portion of earned interest — protections stronger than most agricultural-Midwest states. Consumer complaints run through dhhs.ne.gov. The state also specifically prohibits "solicitation of funeral business in a hospital, nursing home, or similar institution" by funeral establishment staff — a cleanup law passed after abuses in the 1980s — which keeps the state's funeral marketing notably more restrained than elsewhere.

Forest Lawn, Wyuka, and Nebraska's Cemetery Pricing Landscape

Forest Lawn Memorial Park in north Omaha — consecrated in 1885 and containing the graves of Omaha founders, Union Pacific executives, and the Ak-Sar-Ben royalty — prices standard plots at $2,800-$5,200 and premium sections higher. Wyuka Cemetery in Lincoln, established in 1869 and holding Nebraska's state monument to Civil War soldiers, runs in the same band with added premiums for the historic Old Yard. Beyond the metros, Nebraska cemetery pricing falls off sharply: township cemeteries in Cedar, Knox, Boyd, and most Sandhills counties price single plots at $250-$700, and many parish-run Catholic and Lutheran cemeteries quote members a plot for a modest donation. Omaha National Cemetery near Papillion, dedicated in 2016 as the state's third VA cemetery, and the Nebraska Veterans Cemetery in Alliance offer no-cost burial for eligible veterans.

Nebraska Aid to the Aged, Blind, and Disabled (AABD) Burial Allowance

Nebraska operates one of the oldest state-funded burial assistance programs in the country. The Aid to the Aged, Blind, and Disabled program through Nebraska DHHS pays up to $420 toward the funeral expenses of a recipient who dies while on AABD, with Nebraska counties supplementing as they choose — Douglas and Lancaster counties add up to $1,200 to reach a functional disposition minimum. The program requires the total funeral bill not exceed state guidelines and applies before any insurance or third-party payment. For Medicaid recipients, Nebraska allows up to $5,372 in an irrevocable burial contract or $1,500 in a burial fund without affecting eligibility — a planning tool most families learn about only after the funeral bill arrives. Surviving spouses should also file the Social Security lump-sum death payment under 42 U.S.C. § 402(i) ($255). Veterans, regardless of income, should first check eligibility at Omaha National Cemetery or Nebraska Veterans Cemetery in Alliance; burial allowances and plot allowances are administered under VA 38 CFR § 3.1600.

Charges to push back on

Most of the pricing surprises in Nebraska come from a predictable list of add-ons. Look for these specifically when reviewing any funeral home quote:

  • Casket handling fee — Some funeral homes add a surcharge for caskets purchased elsewhere, despite this being illegal under the FTC Funeral Rule
  • Mandatory embalming — Funeral homes may claim embalming is required. In Nebraska, it is almost never legally required. Know your rights
  • Cemetery "perpetual care" fees — An ongoing maintenance charge, typically $200–$1,000, added on top of the plot price
  • Vault/liner surcharge — Cemeteries may require a specific vault brand or type, inflating costs by $500–$2,000+
  • Documentation and filing fees — Death certificates, permits, and filing fees can add $200–$600
  • Weekend/holiday premium — Services held outside business hours may carry a 15–25% surcharge
  • "Package" markups — Bundled packages often include services you don't need. Always compare line-by-line. What funeral homes don't tell you

Your Rights Under the FTC Funeral Rule

Know Your Rights Under the FTC Funeral Rule Every funeral home in Nebraska must comply with the Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule, which protects consumers nationwide. You have the right to: receive an itemized General Price List before making any decisions; choose only the services and products you want; purchase a casket or urn from a third party without penalty; decline embalming unless required by state law for specific circumstances; and receive a written estimate before services are performed.

If you believe a funeral home in Nebraska has violated these rights, you can file a complaint with the FTC at ftc.gov or contact the Funeral Consumers Alliance for guidance. Your state attorney general's office can also assist with consumer protection complaints.

Sandhills Distance and the Rural Transfer Question

Cherry County covers 6,000 square miles with a single funeral home in Valentine. Grant County has 600 residents and none. For families across the Sandhills, the Panhandle, and north-central Nebraska, the relevant question is not which funeral home is cheapest but whether the transfer mileage to the nearest provider will add $300-$700 to a bill that otherwise undercuts the metro average. Some Nebraska funeral homes quote a flat rural transfer fee; others bill $3-$5 per loaded mile. Ask for both rates before the transfer is authorized — over 150 miles the flat fee often saves hundreds.

Questions Families Ask

How much does a funeral cost in Nebraska?

Traditional Nebraska funerals average $6,700 — 15% below the $7,848 national figure. Cremation with service runs about $5,300; direct cremation starts at $1,500, with Omaha's Cremation Society of Nebraska and Cremation Plus advertising $995–$1,295 packages. Forest Lawn Memorial Park in Omaha and Wyuka Cemetery in Lincoln quote plots $2,800–$5,200.

How much cheaper is cremation than burial in Nebraska?

Direct cremation near $1,500 versus a $6,700 traditional funeral plus a $3,000 plot is a $8,200 spread at the high end. Nebraska's 50% cremation rate hovers near the national midpoint. Omaha and Lincoln rates run higher, while the Sandhills and Catholic Platte Valley towns remain burial-dominant.

What rights do Nebraska families have when arranging a funeral?

The FTC Funeral Rule applies. Nebraska adds protections through Revised Statutes 38-1401 through 38-1434 and the Board of Funeral Directing and Embalming at DHHS, including 100% preneed trust requirements and a prohibition on funeral business solicitation in hospitals or nursing homes. Complaints go to dhhs.ne.gov.

Do I have to embalm a loved one in Nebraska?

No. Nebraska does not require embalming in routine circumstances; refrigeration is a valid substitute. A funeral home may require embalming as internal policy for open-casket public viewings but cannot represent that state law requires it unless it does. The FTC Funeral Rule prohibits that misrepresentation.

Is Nebraska cheaper or more expensive than the US average for funerals?

Nebraska's $6,700 traditional average sits 15% below the $7,848 national median. The state runs cheaper than Illinois ($8,500) and comparable to Iowa and Kansas. Omaha (Douglas County) and Lincoln (Lancaster County) price modestly above the state average; rural ranching counties in the west run 10–15% below.

What is the best way to find a cheap funeral home in Nebraska?

Pull three GPLs: one Omaha or Lincoln metro (Heafey Hoffmann Dworak & Cutler, Butherus-Maser-Love), one direct-cremation specialist, and one small-town Nebraska independent (Braman in Norfolk, Kremer in Aurora, Roeder). Funeral Consumers Alliance of Greater Omaha tracks regional pricing. For rural transfers over 150 miles, ask for both the per-mile and flat transfer rate — the flat fee usually saves hundreds.

Practical steps before you sign anything

For anyone planning a funeral in Nebraska now or soon, these steps — in roughly this order — prevent the most common expensive mistakes:

  1. Take a breath. Unless there are legal or medical time constraints, you typically have 24–72 hours before decisions must be finalized.
  2. Request General Price Lists from at least 2–3 funeral homes in your area of Nebraska. They are legally required to provide them. Questions to ask funeral homes
  3. Decide on disposition: cremation in Nebraska or burial in Nebraska. This is the single biggest cost decision.
  4. Choose only the services you need. Embalming, premium caskets, and elaborate arrangements are optional. What funeral homes don't tell you
  5. Explore payment assistance if cost is a concern: Social Security benefits, veteran benefits, Medicaid assistance, or crowdfunding.

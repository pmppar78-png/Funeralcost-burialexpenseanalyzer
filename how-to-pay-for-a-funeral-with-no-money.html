How to Pay for a Funeral With No Money: Every Option Explained

Reviewed: April 2026 Sources: NFDA, FTC, SSA, VA, State Programs Editorial: Our Standards

Losing someone you love is one of the most painful experiences in life. When that loss is compounded by the stress of not knowing how you will pay for a funeral, the burden can feel unbearable. You are not alone in facing this challenge, and there is no shame in needing help.

According to the National Funeral Directors Association, the average cost of a funeral with a viewing and burial in 2026 is approximately $7,848, and that figure does not include the cemetery plot, headstone, or additional fees that can push the total well above $10,000. For millions of American families, these costs represent a significant financial hardship. A 2024 Federal Reserve survey found that roughly 37 percent of adults could not cover an unexpected $400 expense without borrowing or selling something — let alone an expense that is twenty times that amount.

The good news is that there are real, practical options available to every family regardless of income level. From government assistance programs and low-cost alternatives to community support and financing plans, this guide covers every avenue for paying for a funeral when money is tight. We have organized these resources from most immediate to longer-term planning so you can find what is most relevant to your situation right now.

For a complete breakdown of what makes up these costs, see our funeral cost breakdown guide, or use our free funeral cost calculator to estimate expenses in your area.

Government Assistance Programs

Several federal, state, and local government programs exist specifically to help families who cannot afford funeral costs. These programs vary in eligibility requirements and benefit amounts, but they represent some of the most accessible resources available. If you are facing immediate need, start by exploring these options first.

Social Security Lump-Sum Death Benefit ($255)

The Social Security Administration provides a one-time lump-sum death payment of $255 to the surviving spouse or eligible dependent child of a deceased worker who paid Social Security taxes. While $255 is a far cry from covering full funeral costs, it can help offset a portion of expenses and can be applied for at your local Social Security office or by calling 1-800-772-1213. You must file within two years of the death. For a detailed walkthrough of eligibility requirements and the application process, see our Social Security death benefit guide.

Medicaid Funeral Assistance

Medicaid funeral assistance varies significantly from state to state. Some states provide a small funeral benefit for qualifying recipients, typically ranging from $500 to $1,500, though a handful of states offer more generous allowances. In certain states, Medicaid may cover the cost of a basic funeral only if the deceased had no other assets or estate funds available. Other states have no direct Medicaid funeral benefit at all but may have separate programs. Contact your state Medicaid office as soon as possible to find out what benefits are available in your area. Our Medicaid funeral assistance guide breaks down state-by-state benefits so you know exactly what to expect and how to apply.

Veteran Burial Benefits

If the deceased was a military veteran, the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) offers several burial benefits that can substantially reduce funeral costs. These include a burial allowance of up to $2,000 or more for service-connected deaths, a plot allowance for burial in a private cemetery, a free burial in a national cemetery (which includes the gravesite, opening and closing of the grave, and a headstone or marker), and a presidential memorial certificate. The VA also provides a free burial flag. Survivors should file VA Form 21P-530 to claim these benefits. Our comprehensive veteran burial benefits guide explains every benefit available and how to apply. For families of veterans, these benefits can cover a significant portion of funeral costs and should always be explored.

County and Municipal Indigent Burial Programs

Nearly every county in the United States has some form of indigent burial program designed to ensure that no person goes without a dignified disposition, regardless of financial circumstances. These programs are typically administered through the county coroner, medical examiner, or social services department. Eligibility is usually based on the deceased having no assets and no family members able to pay. The level of service provided varies widely — some counties offer only basic cremation, while others provide a simple burial with a graveside service. Contact your county social services office or coroner to learn about the specific program in your area. These programs exist precisely for situations where families have exhausted all other options.

Low-Cost Funeral Options

If government assistance is not available or does not cover enough, choosing a lower-cost funeral arrangement is one of the most effective ways to manage expenses. Modern families have more choices than ever, and a meaningful farewell does not require spending thousands of dollars. Here are the most affordable options available.

Direct Cremation ($1,000 to $2,000)

Direct cremation is the most affordable funeral option available in most areas, typically costing between $1,000 and $2,000. With direct cremation, the body is cremated shortly after death without embalming, viewing, or a formal funeral service. The cremated remains are returned to the family in a basic container, and you are free to hold a memorial service at any time and place of your choosing — at home, in a park, at a place of worship, or anywhere that feels meaningful. Some providers in competitive markets offer direct cremation for as little as $800. This option saves families thousands of dollars compared to a traditional funeral while still allowing for a personalized remembrance. For pricing details in your area, see our direct cremation cost guide.

Direct Burial

Direct burial is the burial equivalent of direct cremation. The body is buried shortly after death in a simple container, without embalming, viewing, or a formal funeral service. Costs typically range from $2,000 to $4,000, depending on the cemetery and location. While more expensive than direct cremation, it is still significantly less than a traditional funeral with all the associated services. Some cemeteries offer lower-cost sections or accept alternative containers instead of expensive caskets. Religious communities sometimes have access to dedicated burial grounds that offer reduced costs. You can compare burial costs in your state using our burial costs by state guide.

Body Donation to Science

Donating a body to a medical school or scientific research program can eliminate funeral costs entirely. Most body donation programs cover all transportation and cremation costs and return the cremated remains to the family after the research period, which typically lasts one to three years. This option provides a meaningful gift to medical education and science while relieving the family of financial burden. Programs at major universities accept donations in most states, though there are eligibility requirements and some conditions may result in the body not being accepted. It is important to register with a program in advance when possible, though some programs accept unregistered donors. Our body donation guide covers the process, eligibility requirements, and how to register.

Home Funerals

A home funeral involves the family caring for the deceased at home, including washing, dressing, and keeping the body for a viewing or vigil before burial or cremation. Home funerals are legal in most states (with some states requiring a funeral director to be involved in certain steps) and can cost as little as $200 to $500 when combined with a direct cremation or natural burial. This deeply personal option was the norm for most of human history and is experiencing a resurgence among families who want a more intimate, hands-on approach to saying goodbye. Many families who choose home funerals report finding the experience profoundly healing. For complete state-by-state legality information and step-by-step guidance, see our home funeral guide.

More Affordable Funeral Alternatives

Beyond these primary low-cost options, there are many other ways to reduce funeral expenses without sacrificing meaning or dignity. These include choosing a memorial service instead of a full funeral (eliminating embalming and casket costs), using a funeral home's least expensive casket or an alternative container, holding the service at a church or community center instead of the funeral home chapel, arranging flowers through a grocery store rather than a florist, and writing your own obituary for free online publication. Our affordable funeral options guide covers more than a dozen strategies for reducing costs while still honoring your loved one beautifully.

Funeral Insurance and Financial Planning

While insurance may not help with an immediate need today, understanding funeral insurance options is essential for families who want to plan ahead and prevent this financial burden from falling on their loved ones in the future. If the deceased had an existing insurance policy, it may also provide funds to cover current funeral expenses.

Final Expense Insurance

Final expense insurance, also known as burial insurance, is a type of small whole life insurance policy specifically designed to cover funeral and end-of-life costs. Policies typically range from $5,000 to $25,000 in coverage, with monthly premiums that are affordable for most seniors. Unlike traditional life insurance, many final expense policies offer simplified or guaranteed acceptance with no medical exam required. If a loved one had a final expense policy at the time of death, the beneficiary should file a claim as soon as possible — most companies process claims within 30 to 60 days, and some offer expedited payment for funeral-related expenses. For a thorough understanding of what these policies cover and how they work, read our final expense insurance guide.

Burial Insurance

Burial insurance and final expense insurance are essentially the same product marketed under different names. If you are planning ahead, comparing burial insurance companies can help you find the most affordable coverage for your age and health status. Premiums vary significantly between providers — a healthy 60-year-old might pay anywhere from $30 to $80 per month for $10,000 in coverage, depending on the company and policy type. Our best burial insurance companies comparison helps you evaluate the top providers side by side, including coverage amounts, premium ranges, acceptance criteria, and payout speed.

Life Insurance Claims

If the deceased had any form of life insurance — through an employer, union, professional association, or individual policy — the death benefit can be used to cover funeral costs. Many people have life insurance through their workplace without their family members being aware of it. Check with the deceased's employer, former employers, professional organizations, and any financial documents or files for evidence of a policy. You can also search the NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator for free. Our life insurance and funeral costs guide explains how to find and claim these benefits quickly.

Comparing Funeral Insurance Options

If you are planning ahead for your own funeral expenses or helping a family member do so, comparing insurance options carefully is critical. The differences in premiums, coverage, waiting periods, and payout terms between companies can save or cost you thousands of dollars over time. Some policies build cash value that you can borrow against, while others are pure protection. Some have graded benefits that limit payouts in the first two to three years, while others provide immediate full coverage. Understanding these distinctions is the key to making a smart choice. Our funeral insurance comparison guide breaks down the differences between final expense, burial, and pre-need insurance so you can choose the right type for your situation.

Crowdfunding and Community Help

When traditional funding sources fall short, turning to your community — both online and offline — can make a significant difference. People are often more willing to help than you might expect, and there is no shame in asking for support during one of life's most difficult moments.

GoFundMe and Online Crowdfunding

Crowdfunding has become one of the most common ways American families cover unexpected funeral costs. Funeral-related campaigns are consistently among the most funded categories on platforms like GoFundMe, with successful campaigns raising an average of $2,000 to $5,000. Some campaigns raise significantly more, particularly when they gain traction on social media. To create an effective campaign, include a heartfelt but honest description of your loved one and the situation, share clear photos, set a realistic goal based on actual funeral costs (use our funeral cost calculator to estimate), and share the campaign on every social media platform and in text messages to friends and family. Every share exponentially increases your reach. For a complete step-by-step guide to creating and promoting a successful funeral fundraiser, see our crowdfunding funeral costs guide.

Community Fundraising

Beyond online crowdfunding, local community fundraising can be remarkably effective. Consider organizing a benefit dinner, bake sale, car wash, or community event. Local businesses may be willing to donate goods, services, or venue space. Some communities have mutual aid networks or community relief funds specifically designated for emergency assistance. Neighborhood associations, social clubs, fraternal organizations like the Elks or Moose Lodge, and labor unions sometimes have emergency benevolent funds that can help members' families with funeral costs. Schools and workplaces often organize collections when a colleague or parent passes away. Do not underestimate the generosity of people who knew and cared about the person who has died.

Religious and Faith Organization Assistance

Many churches, synagogues, mosques, temples, and other religious institutions provide funeral assistance to their members and sometimes to people in the broader community. This assistance can take many forms: direct financial help, use of facilities for services at no charge, volunteer assistance with food and logistics, and connections to low-cost funeral providers. Some denominations have regional or national benevolent funds for member families in financial need. Catholic Charities, the Salvation Army, the St. Vincent de Paul Society, and Jewish Family Services are just a few examples of faith-based organizations that may provide funeral assistance regardless of religious affiliation. Contact local congregations directly to ask about available support.

Funeral Payment Plans and Financing

If you need to proceed with funeral arrangements but cannot pay the full amount upfront, many funeral homes offer financing options that can help spread the cost over time. Understanding these options and comparing prices can save you hundreds or even thousands of dollars.

Funeral Home Payment Plans

An increasing number of funeral homes offer in-house payment plans or work with third-party financing companies to help families manage costs. Terms typically range from 12 to 60 months, with interest rates varying by provider. Some funeral homes, particularly nonprofit and community-based providers, offer interest-free payment arrangements for qualifying families. When discussing payment plans, always ask about the total cost including interest and fees, whether there are prepayment penalties, and what happens if you miss a payment. Get all terms in writing before signing anything. Our funeral payment plans guide explains the different types of financing available and how to negotiate the best terms.

Prepaid Funeral Plans for Future Planning

If you are reading this guide as part of advance planning rather than immediate need, prepaid funeral plans (also known as pre-need arrangements) allow you to pay for your funeral in advance at today's prices. These plans typically lock in current pricing and can be paid in a lump sum or through monthly installments. They can be an effective way to protect your family from future costs and ensure your wishes are followed. However, prepaid plans carry some risks, including the possibility that the funeral home could go out of business or that the plan may not be fully transferable if you move. Always research the provider thoroughly and understand the contract terms. Our prepaid funeral plans guide covers the benefits, risks, and what to look for in a contract.

Comparison Shopping for Funeral Services

One of the most powerful tools available to families trying to control funeral costs is simply comparing prices. Funeral home prices vary dramatically — it is not uncommon to find price differences of $3,000 or more for essentially the same services between providers in the same city. The FTC requires all funeral homes to provide a General Price List (GPL) over the phone or in person, making comparison shopping straightforward. Call at least three funeral homes in your area and ask for their prices on the specific services you need. Our funeral price comparison guide walks you through exactly how to compare costs effectively, what questions to ask, and what to watch out for. Combined with our affordable funeral options strategies, comparison shopping alone can save thousands of dollars.

Know Your Rights — The FTC Funeral Rule

When you are grieving and financially stressed, it is essential to know that federal law protects you from being pressured into spending more than you can afford. The Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule, first enacted in 1984 and updated in subsequent years, gives you specific rights when dealing with funeral homes. Understanding these rights can save you thousands of dollars and prevent you from being taken advantage of during a vulnerable time.

Your Key Consumer Protections

Under the FTC Funeral Rule, every funeral home in the United States must provide you with an itemized General Price List (GPL) that shows the cost of each individual service and product they offer. You have the right to choose only the services you want — funeral homes cannot require you to purchase package deals or bundle unwanted services. You are also allowed to use a casket or urn purchased from a third party (such as an online retailer) without any additional handling fees. Funeral homes cannot charge you for embalming without your permission, except where required by state law for specific circumstances. They must also provide a written statement if they charge a fee for handling a casket purchased elsewhere. For a comprehensive explanation of every protection the Funeral Rule provides, see our FTC Funeral Rule guide.

Protection From Overcharging

Unfortunately, some funeral homes engage in practices that result in families paying far more than necessary. These can include presenting only high-priced options, implying that certain services are legally required when they are not, adding undisclosed fees, or pressuring families to spend beyond their means during an emotional time. Knowing your rights under the Funeral Rule is your strongest defense against these practices. If you believe a funeral home has violated the Funeral Rule, you can file a complaint with the FTC and your state's attorney general. Our funeral overcharging protection guide helps you identify common overcharging tactics and what to do about them.

Understanding Your Consumer Rights in Full

Beyond the federal Funeral Rule, many states have additional consumer protection laws related to funeral pricing and practices. Some states require funeral homes to disclose their prices online, while others mandate specific disclosures about casket markups or embalming requirements. Knowing both your federal and state rights puts you in the strongest position to make informed decisions and keep costs manageable. For a complete overview of your rights as a funeral consumer, including state-specific protections, visit our consumer rights in funeral pricing guide.

You deserve to be treated with honesty and respect during one of the hardest times in your life. Do not hesitate to ask questions, request itemized pricing, and walk away from any funeral provider that pressures you or fails to provide transparent pricing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if you can't afford a funeral?

If you cannot afford a funeral, you have several options. You can apply for government assistance through Social Security, Medicaid, or veteran benefits. You can choose low-cost alternatives like direct cremation (starting around $1,000) or direct burial. Many counties have indigent burial programs for families with no financial resources. Crowdfunding through GoFundMe is another common solution. Funeral homes are also increasingly offering payment plans. In all cases, you have the right under the FTC Funeral Rule to receive an itemized price list and choose only the services you want.

Does Social Security pay for funeral costs?

Social Security provides a one-time lump-sum death benefit of $255, which is paid to a surviving spouse or dependent child. This amount has not been adjusted since 1954 and covers only a small fraction of average funeral costs. The benefit must be applied for within two years of the death. While it is not enough to cover a full funeral, it can help offset a portion of the costs when combined with other assistance. See our Social Security death benefit guide for full details.

How much does a direct cremation cost?

Direct cremation typically costs between $1,000 and $2,000, making it the most affordable disposition option available. This includes basic transportation of the deceased, the cremation process itself, and return of cremated remains in a simple container. There is no embalming, viewing, or funeral ceremony included. Some cremation providers charge as little as $800 in certain areas. You can hold a separate memorial service at any time after the cremation at minimal or no additional cost. See our direct cremation cost guide for pricing in your area.

Can you make payments on a funeral?

Yes, many funeral homes now offer payment plans to help families manage costs over time. Some funeral homes provide in-house financing with monthly installments, while others partner with third-party financing companies. Terms typically range from 12 to 60 months. Interest rates vary, so it is important to ask about all fees and compare options. Some nonprofit funeral homes and religious organizations also offer interest-free payment arrangements for families in need. Our funeral payment plans guide explains all your options.

Does Medicaid pay for funerals?

Medicaid funeral assistance varies significantly by state. Some states provide a small funeral allowance for Medicaid recipients, typically ranging from $500 to $1,500. Other states offer no direct funeral benefit. In many cases, Medicaid may cover funeral costs only if the deceased had no assets or estate to pay for services. Contact your state Medicaid office to learn about specific benefits available in your area. Our Medicaid funeral assistance guide has state-by-state details.

Is GoFundMe a good way to pay for a funeral?

GoFundMe and similar crowdfunding platforms have become one of the most common ways families cover unexpected funeral costs. Funeral campaigns on GoFundMe raise an average of $2,000 to $5,000, though amounts vary widely. To maximize donations, share a heartfelt story about the deceased, include photos, set a realistic goal based on actual funeral costs, and share the campaign widely on social media. Funds are typically available within a few business days. Keep in mind that GoFundMe charges a payment processing fee of about 2.9 percent plus 30 cents per donation. Read our crowdfunding funeral costs guide for a step-by-step walkthrough.

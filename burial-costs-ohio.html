The total cost of burial in Ohio, including all cemetery fees, depends on the service type and the cemetery. Adding up the rows in the table above, a traditional funeral and burial in Ohio comes to roughly $16,190 to $20,690, and the headstone accounts for most of that spread. The $7,100 funeral service row is a bundled package; the basic services fee inside it, the one charge a family cannot decline, had a national median of $2,495 in the NFDA 2023 General Price List Study. The casket estimate is $2,130, within a $710 to $5,680 range, and the FTC Funeral Rule lets you buy the casket from any seller without paying the funeral home a handling fee. Opening and closing the grave is estimated at $1,700, but each Ohio cemetery sets its own charge, anywhere from about $500 to $3,000.

Burial and Direct Cremation Options in Ohio

  • Many families choose cremation instead. Our Ohio cremation estimate puts direct cremation at about $1,700 (a $1,190 to $2,380 range) and cremation with a service at about $5,600, usually with an alternative container rather than a casket.
  • Green burials in Ohio often run about $1,500 to $5,000. Learn about green burial

The gap is large: direct cremation costs about $5,400 less than the funeral service row alone, before a single cemetery charge. That helps explain why this site’s rounded Ohio cremation rate is about 55%, though Ohio still trails the 63.4% national rate NFDA projected for 2025.

Regional Context for Burial in Ohio

Prices still vary inside Ohio. A funeral home in Columbus or Cleveland and one in a small county seat can quote very different totals for the same package, and cemetery plots usually cost more where land is expensive, so the statewide figures above are a starting point rather than a quote. A phone call to two or three Ohio providers is usually enough to see where the price points sit and spot package markups. The FTC Funeral Rule requires a funeral home to give price information over the phone and to hand you its General Price List when you ask about arrangements in person, so you do not have to visit before comparing. If you need a death certificate for banks or insurers, the Ohio Department of Health charges $21.50 per certified copy (ODH ordering page).

Spending Less on Burial in Ohio

Burial costs in Ohio can be managed with smart planning, and some families may find financial help with funeral expenses through public or community programs, especially if they are planning a funeral with no insurance coverage:

  • Look into local assistance programs: Ohio has legal provisions for indigent burials, but support is limited and usually handled by local governments. In some areas, community organizations may also offer emergency financial assistance.
  • Call the township or county clerk: Ohio has many township, county, and fraternal cemeteries that simply do not appear in Google results.
  • Know who pays when there is no money: under Ohio Rev. Code § 9.15, the township or city where the person lived pays for burial or cremation when the body is unclaimed or is claimed by someone with income at or below 150% of the federal poverty line, and the grave must get a marker with the person’s name and date of death.
  • Price direct burial: immediate burial with no viewing or embalming removes the $710 embalming estimate and much of the service package, and every Ohio funeral home has to list its direct burial price on the General Price List.
  • Check benefit eligibility: Eligible family members may receive the Social Security Administration's $255 death benefit, and FEMA may help cover funeral costs after federally declared disasters.

Frequently Asked Questions About Planning Ahead

What is the full price of burial in Ohio?

A full burial in Ohio comes in at about $7,100 for the funeral service, $3,400 for the cemetery plot, $2,130 for a casket, $1,360 for an outer burial container, $1,700 for opening and closing, and $500-$5,000 for a grave marker. For comparison, NFDA's 2023 national median for a funeral with viewing and burial is $8,300, and that figure leaves out the vault and every cemetery charge. Ohio totals generally land between $16,190 and $20,690 depending on choices.

Is a burial vault required in Ohio?

Burial vaults are required by most cemeteries in Ohio as a matter of cemetery policy (to prevent ground settling), though they are rarely mandated by state law. Vault costs in Ohio typically range from $800 to $10,000.

What is the most affordable way to bury someone in Ohio?

Direct burial is the lowest-cost path in Ohio: it skips viewing, ceremony, and embalming. Immediate burial is another simple, lower-cost option with minimal services and no viewing. Green or natural burial is the next rung up, available at a growing number of Ohio cemeteries with biodegradable containers and no embalming.

What is the best way to shop cemeteries in Ohio?

Cemetery costs are separate from funeral home charges, and the grave marker is usually priced separately as well. Notably, township, county, and fraternal cemeteries in Ohio are often only listed through the county clerk and frequently price plots well below commercial memorial parks.

What is the total cost of burial in Ohio including everything?

The first answer above lists each item; in Ohio, cash advance items such as certified death certificates, an obituary notice and flowers are added on top of the $16,190 to $20,690 range. Body preparation for a public viewing usually includes embalming, estimated at about $710 in Ohio (a $500 to $1,500 range), plus cosmetic work, and many Ohio funeral homes require embalming before a public viewing.

Does Ohio have green burial cemeteries?

There are green burial options in parts of Ohio, though coverage is not statewide. Ohio also allows home burial on private property when local zoning approval is obtained. Some Ohio cemeteries have hybrid sections rather than fully dedicated grounds. Check our green burial guide and call local cemetery options to confirm what they currently offer.

What rights do you have when making arrangements with a funeral home in Ohio?

When making arrangements, the funeral provider must give you a General Price List before you select services, as required by the FTC Funeral Rule. This helps you compare funeral arrangements and avoid paying for items you do not want.

Who helps with paperwork after a death in Ohio?

The funeral director usually helps obtain certified copies of the death certificate. Family members often need those copies for a bank, insurance claims, and the Social Security Administration.

What paperwork is needed for cremation in Ohio?

If cremation is chosen, the funeral director or funeral professionals will require a Cremation Authorization Form signed by all legal next of kin before cremated remains can be released or disposition can proceed. If there is disagreement among legal next relatives, the process can be delayed until it is resolved.

How can you pay for funeral costs in Ohio without leaving the bill to relatives?

Planning ahead can make costs easier to manage through burial insurance, final expense insurance, or a payable-on-death bank account. These options can help cover funeral ceremony expenses without placing the full burden on family members, and if you buy a preneed contract from an Ohio funeral home, ask in writing whether it is funded through a trust or an insurance policy and what happens to the money if you move or cancel.

Are there veterans' benefits that help with funeral expenses in Ohio?

Eligible veterans and their families may qualify for veterans affairs burial benefits, including a burial allowance in some cases. Ask the funeral director and staff whether these benefits apply and what documentation is needed.

Burial rules and help in Ohio

For a license check or complaint about a funeral home arranging a burial in Ohio, call the Ohio Board of Embalmers and Funeral Directors at 614-466-4252; Ohio cemeteries can have a separate regulator.

Ohio is not on the Funeral Consumers Alliance's list of nine states that make a funeral director part of every burial, which leaves an Ohio family free to settle the plot, the opening of the grave and the accepted container with the cemetery itself. Township cemeteries are one of the cheaper places to start: Ohio Rev. Code § 517.07 has the board of township trustees sell lots at a reasonable price as public demand requires, and a buyer who meets the terms of sale may receive a deed, recorded by the township fiscal officer at the buyer's expense.

A burial in Ohio goes ahead once the permit is issued; for cremation, a 24-hour wait applies (Ohio Rev. Code § 4717.23).

In Ohio, the township or city where the person lived pays for the burial when the body is unclaimed or the claimant's household earns no more than 150% of the poverty line, and the state embalmers board repays part of that cost while its fund lasts (Ohio Rev. Code § 9.15). (Source: Ohio Rev. Code § 9.15; Ohio Admin. Code 4717-17-06.)

An Ohio veteran's grave in a national cemetery costs the family nothing for the plot, opening and closing, or marker. In Ohio, VA national cemeteries accepting burials: Dayton National Cemetery (937-268-2221); Ohio Western Reserve National Cemetery (330-335-3069); state veterans cemetery: Ohio Veterans Home Cemetery (419-625-2454) (source: VA National Cemetery Administration).

Next steps for a burial in Ohio

  1. Get the funeral home's General Price List and the Ohio cemetery's own price sheet before you agree to a burial.
  2. Ask the Ohio cemetery whether it requires an outer burial container before you shop for one.
  3. Ask the Ohio township whether it owns a cemetery where an indigent burial can be placed.
  4. For a veteran, ask Dayton National Cemetery about eligibility before buying a private plot.
  5. A complaint call to 614-466-4252 is free if a funeral home misleads you about burial costs.

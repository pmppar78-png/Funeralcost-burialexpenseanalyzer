Cremation Costs in Washington (2026)

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Last updated: October 29, 2026 Last reviewed: October 30, 2026 What changed: Washington cremation page revised to cover family-directed care, county indigent help and King County timing, with unsourced flat-rate and turnaround claims removed. Cremation Rate: 76%

Cremation in Washington costs less than burial, yet providers can still be thousands of dollars apart. In Washington, direct cremation starts around $2,100 while cremation with a full service averages $6,800, a gap of $4,700. Below we cover what each price tier includes and how a 76% cremation rate shapes the market. One cost a Washington family can usually avoid is a cemetery niche, since RCW 68.50.130 lets cremated remains be placed on private property with the owner's consent, or on public lands or waters with approval from the agency in charge.

Authorization and Urns in Washington

Two Washington rules to know first:

  • Authorization: Written authorization from the legal next of kin is required; the Washington rules are in RCW 70.58A.210.
Urns can come from anywhere. The FTC Funeral Rule lets a family in Washington supply its own, and a funeral home that refuses one can be reported to the Washington State Department of Licensing's Funeral and Cemetery Board.

Paying Less for a Washington Cremation

Ways a Washington family can lower the bill:

  • Collect written General Price Lists from at least three Washington providers; the direct cremation line is where they differ most.
  • Handle more of it yourselves: Washington lets families act without a funeral director once the death is registered and a burial-transit permit is issued (RCW 70.58A.210), so a family can deal with the crematory directly; the Funeral Consumers Alliance does not list Washington among the nine states that require a funeral director.
  • Keeping the ashes costs nothing extra; an eligible veteran's urn can go to Tahoma National Cemetery, Vancouver Barracks National Cemetery or the state veterans cemetery at Medical Lake at no charge for the space.
  • If nobody can pay, ask the county: it must arrange disposition of an unclaimed indigent person (RCW 36.39.030) and pay for an indigent veteran (RCW 73.08.070). See Veteran benefits, Medicaid assistance and other programs.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the price of cremation in Washington?

We estimate about $2,100 for a Washington direct cremation, which pays for transfer, cremation and delivery of the ashes, and about $6,800 when a viewing or ceremony comes first.

What cremation choices do Washington families have?

Most Washington families choose between direct cremation at about $2,100, with no viewing or ceremony, and a funeral followed by cremation at about $6,800, a $4,700 difference. Washington also permits natural organic reduction and alkaline hydrolysis (RCW 68.50.110), so ask whether a provider offers either and what it costs.

What does Washington law require before cremation?

Washington's rule is in RCW 70.58A.210, and in King County the medical examiner must also authorize every disposition before release. Washington sets no waiting period before cremation; families may act without a funeral director once the death is registered and a burial-transit permit issued.

Under the FTC Funeral Rule, a Washington provider that offers direct cremation must make an alternative container available, so you are not required to buy a casket.

If a crematory or funeral home mishandled these steps, complain to the Washington State Department of Licensing (360-664-1555).

Where can ashes be scattered in Washington?

On private land, get the owner's permission; in a national park, ask the park, since many require a free permit, and check any local ordinance. Others avoid the permit question by placing the urn in a columbarium niche, burying it (an eligible veteran can go to Tahoma National Cemetery) or turning some of the ashes into memorial jewelry.

What explains Washington's 76% cremation rate?

Washington combines the factors NFDA links to high cremation rates. About 37% of Washington adults are religiously unaffiliated (Pew Research Center, 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study), and only 54.6% of U.S.-born residents live in the state where they were born (Census Bureau, 2023 American Community Survey).

Washington has also widened the options. In 2019 it became the first state to legalize natural organic reduction, or human composting, and it also allows alkaline hydrolysis; both took effect May 1, 2020 (ESSB 5001; RCW 68.50.110). The 76% rate on this page compares with NFDA's 63.4% national projection for 2025.

How long does the Washington cremation process take?

By the Cremation Association of North America's account, the cremation itself lasts from half an hour to more than two hours, and the remains then cool and are processed. It cannot begin before the Washington cremation permit, and in King County the medical examiner's authorization is processed within 24 hours on business days; its $100 fee is billed to the funeral home. Direct cremation ($2,100) has the shortest timeline because no viewing or ceremony is involved.

Can a Washington family have a funeral and still cremate?

Yes. Visitation and a funeral can come first, for an estimated $6,800 in Washington.

What King County charges to clear a cremation

In Washington the medical examiner's review is set county by county, and King County's is one of the strictest. Every death in the county needs a disposition authorization from the Medical Examiner's Office before the body is released for burial or cremation, wherever that takes place. The fee has been $100 since January 1, 2024, up from $70, and the office bills the funeral home (King County Medical Examiner; checked October 29, 2026).

  • Timing: requests are processed within 24 hours on business days. Requests that arrive after 2:30 p.m. wait until the next business day.
  • What is checked: staff review the cause and manner of death on the death certificate to make sure the death is not one the office should investigate.

Washington cremation oversight and county help

The Washington State Department of Licensing (360-664-1555) oversees crematories and the funeral homes that arrange cremations, and its Funeral and Cemetery Board hears complaints.

Washington does not appear on the Funeral Consumers Alliance list of nine states restricting family-directed care, so relatives holding the cremation permit can arrange things with a crematory themselves.

There is no minimum wait in Washington law. Once the death is registered and the burial-transit permit is issued, a cremation can proceed with or without a funeral director (RCW 70.58A.210).

County help

County commissioners must arrange the disposition of an indigent person whose body is not claimed by relatives or a church, and counties pay for an indigent veteran's disposition (RCW 36.39.030; RCW 73.08.070). King County runs its indigent remains program through the medical examiner. Social Security's one-time $255, payable to an eligible spouse or child, pays for only a small part of a $2,100 direct cremation.

Veterans

An eligible veteran's urn can go into a columbarium or in-ground site with no charge for the space. In Washington that means Tahoma National Cemetery (425-413-9614), Vancouver Barracks National Cemetery (503-273-5250), which now takes cremated remains only, or the Washington State Veterans Cemetery at Medical Lake (509-299-6280); details are at cem.va.gov.

Washington to-do list

  1. Ask two or more Washington providers for written prices, and check what each one's direct cremation figure covers.
  2. Find out which office issues the cremation permit in the county where the death occurred, and how quickly it acts.
  3. If no one can pay, call the county about its indigent remains program before booking anything.
  4. Keep the Department of Licensing number, 360-664-1555, handy before paying any cremation provider.

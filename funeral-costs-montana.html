Funeral Costs in Montana: What Families Pay in 2026

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, FTC Funeral Rule Region: Mountain

Montana sits at the high end of the American cremation curve — 72% of the state chooses fire over earth, third-highest in the country. A traditional funeral in Billings or Great Falls averages $7,100, cremation with a service lands near $5,600, and direct cremation starts around $1,600. But the numbers on the page mask the real story: the 147-mile drive between a rural death and the nearest crematory, the permissive home-burial statute that lets a Gallatin County rancher bury a spouse in the north pasture, and the handful of funeral homes — Dahl in Billings, Smith in Missoula, Whitefield Mortuary up in the Flathead — that set the pricing floor most families eventually anchor to.

The 72% Cremation State: How Montana's Disposition Culture Shapes Price

Montana's 72% cremation rate is not a number you arrive at by accident. Harsh winters make wintertime interment difficult across much of the state — many Montana cemeteries stop digging between November and April and temporarily hold the deceased in a receiving vault, which adds its own $400-$900 line item. Extended families scattered across a rural state find cremation logistically simpler when a memorial service might happen in Seattle, Denver, or Minneapolis weeks later. And the state has few Catholic enclaves on the scale of the Upper Midwest, so the sacramental preference for burial applies less uniformly.

The pricing effect is measurable. Direct-cremation specialists like Cremation Society of Montana (Missoula and Billings locations) advertise flat-rate $995–$1,295 packages — below the state direct-cremation average of $1,600 — because volume and streamlined operations let them compete aggressively. Full-service firms that want cremation business have had to publish separate, stripped-down cremation GPLs. Traditional-burial families in Montana can sometimes negotiate harder than families in lower-cremation states, because the provider knows casket-and-vault revenue is not guaranteed if the family walks.

Home Burial on Montana Private Land — Still Permitted, Rarely Used

Montana is one of the most permissive states in the country for private-land burial. Outside incorporated city limits, Montana law does not require a licensed funeral director to conduct a family burial on land the family owns, so long as the county sanitarian is notified and the standard setback from water wells and property lines is observed. A handful of ranching families in Madison, Beaverhead, Powder River, and Sanders counties still use family plots — the practice is uncommon but legal, and it can eliminate the cemetery plot ($3,200 average), vault ($1,200+), and opening/closing fee ($900-$1,400) from the final bill entirely.

The paperwork is narrower than families expect: a death certificate, a burial transit permit from the county clerk and recorder, and a simple sanitarian sign-off in most counties. Practical constraints matter more than legal ones — frozen ground from late October through May makes winter burial impractical without heavy equipment, and a family plot recorded with the county will encumber the land title permanently. Funeral Consumers Alliance of Montana (based in Missoula) walks families through the process free of charge.

Montana Board of Funeral Service and Preneed Trust Rules

The Montana Board of Funeral Service, housed within the Department of Labor and Industry in Helena, licenses funeral directors, embalmers, and funeral establishments under Title 37, Chapter 19 of Montana Code Annotated. Preneed funeral contracts in Montana must place 100% of the principal into trust — a stricter standard than most Rocky Mountain states — and the statute gives the buyer the right to cancel and receive a full refund of principal plus earned interest. If a funeral home misrepresents the law, inflates non-declinable fees beyond its own GPL, or fails to honor a preneed contract, the Board takes formal complaints at dli.mt.gov and can suspend or revoke licenses.

One Montana wrinkle worth knowing: the state allows a surviving spouse or next-of-kin to execute a simple written authorization rather than requiring a notarized document for most disposition decisions. That small procedural ease matters when a death happens on a weekend in a county without a 24-hour courthouse.

Montana Veterans Cemeteries and Medicaid Funeral Assistance

Veterans in Montana have access to two state veterans cemeteries — the Montana State Veterans Cemetery at Fort Harrison near Helena, and the Eastern Montana Veterans Cemetery north of Miles City — both offering no-cost burial for eligible veterans including the plot, opening/closing, vault, headstone, and perpetual care. The Yellowstone National Cemetery near Laurel, dedicated in 2014, serves south-central Montana. For eligible veterans the combined benefit eliminates $4,000–$6,000 of burial cost.

Montana Medicaid does not pay directly for funerals, but the state's Supplemental Nutrition Assistance and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families programs coordinate through county offices of public assistance for limited burial help when a decedent was receiving benefits and the estate cannot cover minimum disposition costs. The help is modest — typically $800–$1,500, capped — and comes with strict limits on casket value and ceremony spending. Counties with higher poverty rates (Big Horn, Glacier, Roosevelt) fund pauper burials when the state program is exhausted.

The line items that add up fast

Many Montana families are surprised by charges that appear after the initial quote. Here are the most common hidden funeral fees to watch for:

  • Casket handling fee — Some funeral homes add a surcharge for caskets purchased elsewhere, despite this being illegal under the FTC Funeral Rule
  • Mandatory embalming — Funeral homes may claim embalming is required. In Montana, it is almost never legally required. Know your rights
  • Cemetery "perpetual care" fees — An ongoing maintenance charge, typically $200–$1,000, added on top of the plot price
  • Vault/liner surcharge — Cemeteries may require a specific vault brand or type, inflating costs by $500–$2,000+
  • Documentation and filing fees — Death certificates, permits, and filing fees can add $200–$600
  • Weekend/holiday premium — Services held outside business hours may carry a 15–25% surcharge
  • "Package" markups — Bundled packages often include services you don't need. Always compare line-by-line. What funeral homes don't tell you

Buyer Protections to Remember

Know Your Rights Under the FTC Funeral Rule Every funeral home in Montana must comply with the Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule, which protects consumers nationwide. You have the right to: receive an itemized General Price List before making any decisions; choose only the services and products you want; purchase a casket or urn from a third party without penalty; decline embalming unless required by state law for specific circumstances; and receive a written estimate before services are performed.

If you believe a funeral home in Montana has violated these rights, you can file a complaint with the FTC at ftc.gov or contact the Funeral Consumers Alliance for guidance. Your state attorney general's office can also assist with consumer protection complaints.

Rural Distance and the Montana Mileage Surcharge

The real Montana funeral pricing story is geography. Between Jordan (county seat of Garfield County, population 350) and the nearest full-service crematory is roughly 140 miles one way. Between Ekalaka in the far southeast and Billings is 150. These are not edge cases — roughly a third of Montana counties do not have a funeral home inside the county line. Transportation line items that read as inconsequential in Maryland or Rhode Island — $3–$5 per loaded mile — add $400–$900 to a Montana bill when the transfer covers 200 miles of gravel and US-212. Families in Garfield, Petroleum, Golden Valley, and Wibaux counties should ask for the per-mile rate before authorizing transfer, because some providers quote a flat rural transfer fee that works out cheaper than mileage on long hauls.

Questions Worth Asking First

What does a typical Montana funeral cost in 2026?

A traditional Montana funeral averages $7,100 — 10% below the $7,848 national figure. Cremation with a service is about $5,600; direct cremation starts near $1,600. Dahl Funeral Chapel in Billings, Smith Funeral Chapels in Missoula, and Whitefield Mortuary in the Flathead quote in that band. Yellowstone and Gallatin county prices sit above the state average; Hi-Line towns price below. Always pull an itemized GPL before committing.

How much cheaper is cremation than burial in Montana?

Substantially. Direct cremation near $1,600 versus a traditional funeral at $7,100 plus a cemetery plot is a $6,000+ spread. Montana's 72% cremation rate is the third-highest in the country, and flat-rate specialists like Cremation Society of Montana in Missoula and Billings advertise $995–$1,295 packages — well below the state average.

What are my consumer rights at Montana funeral homes?

The FTC Funeral Rule applies in full. Montana adds protections through Title 37 Chapter 19 MCA and the Montana Board of Funeral Service in Helena, including a 100% preneed trust requirement and a right of cancellation with full refund of principal plus interest. Complaints go to the Department of Labor and Industry at dli.mt.gov.

What is the best way to reduce funeral expenses in Montana?

If the family owns rural land outside city limits, a permitted Montana home burial eliminates the plot, vault, and opening/closing fees — legal under MCA with sanitarian notification. Veterans should use the Montana State Veterans Cemetery at Fort Harrison, Eastern Montana Veterans Cemetery near Miles City, or Yellowstone National Cemetery at Laurel. Cremation Society of Montana prices direct cremation around $1,000. Compare the per-mile transport rate against any flat rural transfer fee — on long hauls the flat rate often saves several hundred dollars.

Do I have to embalm a loved one in Montana?

No. Montana does not require embalming in routine circumstances. Refrigeration is accepted for the timelines most funerals involve. If a funeral home claims state law requires it, ask them to cite the MCA section — the FTC Funeral Rule prohibits that representation unless it is accurate.

How do funeral costs in Montana compare to other states?

Montana's $7,100 traditional average sits 10% below the $7,848 US median. The state runs noticeably cheaper than Colorado ($8,000) or Nevada ($8,000) and close to Idaho and Wyoming. Billings, Bozeman, and Missoula price above the state average; Hi-Line and eastern Montana counties price below.

What is the least expensive way to handle a funeral in Montana?

Direct cremation at $995–$1,600 is the commercial floor. A legal Montana home funeral with rural-land burial cuts that further — a straight pine box, transit permit, and family labor can bring the total near $500. For veterans, Fort Harrison or Yellowstone National Cemetery burial is no-cost.

What is the best way to find a cheap funeral home in Montana?

Call Funeral Consumers Alliance of Montana (Missoula) for their price survey. Compare at least three GPLs — one from a full-service chain, one from a Cremation Society of Montana branch, and one from a small-town independent. In rural counties without a local provider, the next-county funeral home quoting a flat rural transfer fee often beats the home-county provider charging per-mile.

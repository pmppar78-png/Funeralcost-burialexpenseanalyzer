Planning Ahead for Aging Parents: Wishes, Documents and Costs

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Last updated: October 24, 2026 Last reviewed: October 24, 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, Government Sources Editorial: Our Standards

Talking with a parent about their funeral can feel impossible until it isn't optional. Families who have one or two calm conversations ahead of time usually spend less and argue less later. This guide gives you a conversation plan, a short list of papers to locate, and the benefits worth checking now.

A conversation plan that doesn't feel morbid

  • Use a natural opening: a friend's funeral, updating a will, or a news story about funeral prices.
  • Ask about preferences, not logistics: burial or cremation, religious or not, big or small, where.
  • Then ask the money question gently: "Have you set anything aside, or bought a plan or policy for this?"
  • Write it down and share it with siblings so no one is guessing later.

Papers to find now

  1. Any prepaid funeral contract or burial insurance policy, with the provider's name.
  2. Life insurance policies and beneficiaries.
  3. Military discharge papers (DD-214), which are needed for veterans' burial benefits.
  4. Deed to a cemetery plot, if one was bought.
  5. Will, power of attorney, and health care proxy.
  6. A list of accounts, with where the passwords are kept.

Benefits to check while you can ask questions

  • Social Security: a one-time $255 payment may go to a surviving spouse or eligible child, and survivors may qualify for monthly benefits (SSA: Survivors benefits).
  • Veterans: burial in a national cemetery, a government headstone, and burial allowances for some veterans (VA.gov: Veterans burial allowance). See our veteran benefits guide.
  • Medicaid and state help: some states help with burial costs for low-income residents; see our Medicaid guide.

Shop while there's no rush

Funeral homes must give you price information over the phone if you ask, without requiring your name or address first (Funeral Consumers Alliance: FTC Funeral Rule), and many will email a price list. For context, NFDA's 2023 national medians were $8,300 for a funeral with viewing and burial and $6,280 with cremation (NFDA 2024 Cremation & Burial Report (2023 GPL Study data)). Collect two or three general price lists now and keep them with the papers above.

Planning with a parent: the conversation, the folder, and the number

This page is for adult children who still have time. It is not the first-48-hours list. That list is the funeral planning checklist. The months-ahead legal file is the end-of-life planning checklist. Here the job is to learn what your parent wants and to put the papers where you can reach them.

How to start without making it a crisis

Pick a normal afternoon. Say that you want to be useful, not that you are worried they are dying. One question is enough for the first talk: "If I had to call a funeral home, do you want burial or cremation?" Write the answer down and read it back. The second talk can cover a church, a cemetery, and whether they want a viewing. People answer more clearly when they are not asked to design an entire funeral in one hour.

The folder

A single folder, paper or a shared drive they have already shown you how to open, should hold: the will's location, the power of attorney, the health care proxy, a list of accounts with institutions but not necessarily every password, insurance policy numbers, the veteran's discharge papers if they served, and the funeral note. Passwords to email can wait until they want to share them. The discharge paper (DD-214) is the document the VA will ask for, and it is harder to replace than a bank login. Our veteran burial benefits guide says what that paper unlocks, including a no-charge government marker.

The funding number, sourced

Ask what they have already set aside. Then show them one page, not a sales brochure. The NFDA 2023 medians, latest available, are $8,300 for a viewing and burial without the vault or cemetery, $6,280 for a viewing and cremation, and $2,645 or $2,750 for direct cremation depending on who supplies the container. If they have more than that in a payable-on-death account, they may not need a burial policy. If they do not, the burial insurance for seniors page is the age-banded explanation, and the funeral insurance guide is the plain-language overview. This parent-planning page does not pick a carrier.

What you should not do yet

Do not prepay a funeral home until you have read the cancellation and transfer terms, which are covered on the prepaid plans guide. Do not put your name on their bank account as a joint owner just to "make it easier." Joint ownership can create gift and creditor problems a payable-on-death form does not. Do not promise siblings a role you cannot explain. If you are the health care proxy, say so. If your brother is the executor, say that too. The estate-planning cost guide separates those jobs.

A script for the second visit

  1. "I wrote down cremation and St. Luke's. Is that still right?"
  2. "Where is the DD-214, and may I scan it?"
  3. "Who should the hospital call at 2 a.m, and who should they not call?"
  4. "Is there a policy or an account meant for the funeral, and where is the statement?"
  5. "Do you want me to compare two funeral homes now, while you can look at the price lists with me?"

If they say they do not want to discuss it, stop. Leave the folder and try again in a month. A forced conversation produces a plan nobody will follow.

Comparing two homes while a parent can still read the list

The useful visit is the one your parent attends, even by speakerphone. Ask each funeral home to email the General Price List. Federal rules require a home to tell you prices and to hand over that list. Sit together and circle only the lines they want: transfer, a direct cremation, or a viewing. Write the total in the folder beside the NFDA 2023 medians so a package price has something honest next to it. Those are national medians; a General Price List from a home in your parent's town will be closer to the real bill. They are national figures, not a quote for your parent's city.

Decide who may change the plan later. The health care agent is not always the person who will pay. The person who will sign the funeral contract should be the one who heard the prices. Tell siblings the date of the call and send them both lists. A parent who has named a church and a song has done the part that is hard to guess. The price comparison can be repeated in a year if they move.

This parent-planning guide does not estimate a lawyer's fee for the will. The estate-planning cost page explains why a national fee is not printed here. What you can finish without that fee is the folder, the two price lists, and the names of who calls whom. Those steps were reviewed for this parent guide on October 22, 2026.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I talk to my parents about funeral plans?

While they are healthy and there is no urgency. A friend's funeral or a will update is a natural opening. Waiting has a practical cost: a will, a power of attorney and a health care proxy can only be signed while a parent still understands what they are signing, and those are the papers this guide asks you to find.

Many parents have not done this yet. In NFDA's 2025 Consumer Awareness and Preferences Study, 19.4% of respondents said they had both pre-planned and prepaid their funeral, so most families still have the conversation ahead of them. If a parent does buy a prepaid plan, keep a copy of the contract with the other papers.

What documents should we find ahead of time?

Prepaid funeral contracts, insurance policies, military discharge papers (DD-214), cemetery deeds, the will and powers of attorney, and a list of accounts.

Can I get funeral prices without visiting?

Yes. The FTC Funeral Rule requires funeral homes to give price information over the phone, and many will email their price lists on request.

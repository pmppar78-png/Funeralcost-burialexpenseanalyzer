Cremation Costs in Kansas (2026)

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Last updated: October 29, 2026 Last reviewed: October 30, 2026 What changed: Kansas cremation page rewritten, with the board's scattering advice, coroner permits and the unclaimed-body rules. Cremation Rate: 50%

For a Kansas family, the main cremation decision is usually whether to hold a service first. Our estimate puts direct cremation at about $1,500 and cremation with a full funeral at about $5,300, a difference of roughly $3,800. About half of Kansas deaths, 50% by the rate we use, now end in cremation. Below are the prices, the state's container and permit rules, scattering guidance from the state board, and what happens when no one claims the body.

Before Signing With a Kansas Provider

Questions to settle with a Kansas provider:

  • Read the direct cremation line. It must state what is included; in Kansas that should mean transport, the cremation and a basic container for the ashes.
  • Choose the container. The FTC Funeral Rule (16 CFR 453.3(b)) requires a Kansas provider to allow an unfinished wood box or another alternative container instead of a casket.
  • Ask about timing. Find out when the ashes will be ready, how they come back, and whether the price includes a certified death certificate.
Kansas families can use an urn from any seller, or one they already have. The FTC Funeral Rule bars the provider from refusing it or charging extra, and the State Board of Mortuary Arts is the place to complain.

Wichita and Small-Town Cremation Prices

About half of Kansas families now choose cremation, and on our estimates the price gap between direct cremation and burial is wide everywhere in the state. Quotes still differ between Wichita-area providers and small-town funeral homes, so if you live outside a city, it can pay to ask for one quote from a city provider as well as the local home.

Spending Less on a Kansas Cremation

Two ways to keep a Kansas cremation affordable:

  • Ask for written prices. Get General Price Lists from at least three Kansas providers and compare the direct cremation figure on each.
  • Look at the standalone price. A Kansas funeral home may offer direct cremation within a package, but it must also list it separately, and that separate line is the one to compare.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is a Kansas cremation?

On our estimates a Kansas direct cremation costs around $1,500, while a cremation that follows a funeral costs around $5,300.

What types of cremation are available in Kansas?

Kansas families usually pick among direct cremation ($1,500), with no viewing or ceremony; cremation followed by a memorial service; and traditional cremation ($5,300), with a full funeral first. K.S.A. 65-1760 defines an alternative container as a combustible, closable, leak-resistant and rigid receptacle other than a casket.

What does Kansas law require before cremation?

Kansas law sets no mandatory waiting period (K.S.A. 65-1762; 65-2426a). Instead, the crematory must have a signed authorization form and a coroner's permit before it can cremate.

If a crematory or funeral home mishandles these steps, complain to the Kansas State Board of Mortuary Arts (785-296-3980).

Can ashes be scattered in Kansas?

Usually, yes. Kansas has no scattering statute. The State Board of Mortuary Arts advises getting the landowner's permission and thinking about neighbors on adjoining land, and it warns that scattering at federal parks and waterways is illegal without authorization. At National Park Service sites in Kansas, 36 CFR 2.62 allows it only by permit or in a designated area.

For a fixed place to visit, a city or county cemetery with a scattering garden is the simplest choice. The board (785-296-3980) licenses the state's crematories and funeral homes.

Why are Kansas families choosing cremation?

Cost explains much of it: by our estimate cremation is about $5,100 cheaper than a traditional burial in Kansas, and it lets the family choose the memorial date. Kansas, at 50%, trails the 63.4% national figure NFDA projected for 2025 by 13.4 points.

How long does cremation take in Kansas?

The cremation takes 2 to 3 hours. Direct cremation ($1,500) is the quickest overall, because only the coroner's permit and the authorization stand between death and cremation; Kansas sets no fixed waiting period (K.S.A. 65-1762; 65-2426a).

Can a Kansas family have a funeral and still cremate?

Yes. A Kansas family can hold a full funeral with the body present and cremate afterwards, for about $5,300 on our estimates (typically $4,240 to $6,890). With no ceremony at all, direct cremation is about $1,500, less than a third of that.

Another common route is direct cremation followed by a memorial gathering, which costs little beyond the venue. Either way, the crematory needs the signed authorization and the coroner's permit first (K.S.A. 65-1762; 65-2426a).

The Kansas coroner's permit to cremate

Kansas will not allow a cremation for a death that happened in the state until a coroner's permit to cremate has been executed. A faxed or electronically signed copy of the permit is legal authorization, which spares families a wait for paper to travel between offices. Knowingly cremating without one carries a fine of up to $500 (K.S.A. 65-2426a; checked October 29, 2026).

The crematory checks for two documents before it starts: that coroner's permit and a cremation authorization form signed by the authorizing agent. The form names who will collect the ashes and includes the agent's statement that the body holds no pacemaker or other hazardous implant (K.S.A. 65-1762).

Kansas's board, unclaimed bodies and veterans cemeteries

The Kansas State Board of Mortuary Arts (785-296-3980) is the Kansas licensing board for crematories and funeral homes, and the number to ring with a complaint.

Kansas is not one of the nine states that, by the Funeral Consumers Alliance count, require a funeral director. A Kansas crematory will still ask for the death certificate, the permit and the next of kin's signed authorization.

No waiting period is required, but the crematory must have a signed authorization form and a coroner's permit (K.S.A. 65-1762; 65-2426a).

When no one claims the body

A Kansas coroner may cremate an unclaimed body under K.S.A. 22a-215. The state assistance program pays if the person qualified under K.S.A. 39-713d; otherwise the county general fund pays. (Source: K.S.A. 22a-215; K.S.A. 39-713d.) Social Security's separate $255 payment goes to a surviving spouse or child who applies within two years.

Burial in a national cemetery stays open to a cremated Kansas veteran at no charge for the space or the headstone. Kansas options include Leavenworth National Cemetery (913-727-1376), Fort Leavenworth National Cemetery (913-684-4348, cremated remains only) and Fort Scott National Cemetery (913-212-4612); see cem.va.gov.

Kansas checklist

  1. Find out if certified copies of the death certificate and the state permit are priced separately.
  2. Ask how quickly the coroner in the county of death usually issues the permit.
  3. If the person received public assistance, ask the coroner whether the K.S.A. 39-713d program will pay.
  4. Report misstated prices to the board at 785-296-3980.

Funeral Flowers: Arrangements, Etiquette and Costs

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Last updated: October 22, 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, Government Sources Editorial: Our Standards

Flowers are one of the few funeral expenses that can come from two directions at once: the family orders them, and friends send them. That makes it easy to pay twice, or to pay a markup without noticing. This guide covers who usually buys what, how the funeral home can bill for flowers, and simple ways to spend less without looking like you did.

Who usually buys which arrangements

  • The immediate family typically orders the casket spray or the arrangement beside the urn.
  • Friends, coworkers, and organizations usually send standing sprays, wreaths, or plants, either to the funeral home or to the family's home.
  • Customs vary. Some religious traditions discourage funeral flowers; check before ordering. Our guides to Jewish and Muslim funerals cover this.

How flowers show up on the funeral bill

If the funeral home orders flowers for you, they are usually a cash advance item, meaning something the home buys from a third party on your behalf. The FTC Funeral Rule lists flowers among typical cash advance items (FTC Funeral Rule, 16 CFR Part 453). According to the FTC, a funeral home that adds a service fee to a cash advance item must disclose in writing that it does so (though not the amount), and must tell you about any refunds, discounts, or rebates from the supplier (FTC: Funeral Costs and Pricing Checklist).

What this means for you: ask whether the home marks up flowers. If it does, you can order directly from a florist and have them delivered to the funeral home.

Ways to spend less

  1. Order one family piece and let friends' arrangements fill the room.
  2. Ask for seasonal flowers. Florists can often do more with what's in stock.
  3. Reuse arrangements: move service flowers to the reception or graveside, then give them to a hospital or nursing home that accepts donations.
  4. If you'd prefer donations, write "In lieu of flowers, please consider a gift to…" in the obituary and name the charity.

About prices

We didn't find a current, methodical national survey of funeral flower prices, so we don't print one. Ask two florists for quotes on the same arrangement (size, flower types, delivery) and compare that with the funeral home's cash-advance price.

Funeral flowers: who sends what, and how not to get billed twice

This funeral flower guide is about etiquette and the order form, not a national price survey. Florists do not file a General Price List with anyone, and the NFDA 2023 study did not price flowers. Ask two shops for the same arrangement, in writing, the way you would ask a funeral home for a GPL.

The pieces, and who traditionally sends them

The casket spray is the large arrangement on the casket. Immediate family usually orders it, because it is the one piece that has to match the service and because the funeral home will often place a spray automatically if nobody says otherwise. Standing sprays and wreaths are what friends, coworkers, and congregations send. They stand on easels beside the casket. Baskets and vases are easier to carry to the family's house afterward. A lid insert or a single rose is a small piece for a child or for a family that asked for restraint.

If the obituary says "in lieu of flowers," send the donation, not a spray. A standing piece that arrives anyway can embarrass the family and still costs the sender. When the family has not said, a modest basket from friends and a spray from the household is the usual split. Red roses are not required. White and the family's own preference are both fine. Religious services sometimes limit flowers at the altar. Ask the church or mosque before you order a piece that cannot be placed.

How to order without the markup you cannot see

  1. Call the funeral home and ask whether a casket spray is already on the contract. If it is, do not let a relative order a second one.
  2. Ask the funeral home which local shops already deliver there, and then call one shop that is not on that list. You want two prices for the same description: "standing spray, about three feet, delivered by 10 a.m."
  3. Put the deceased's full name, the funeral home, the service time, and a short card message in the order. Flowers without a name get set aside.
  4. Ask what happens to the flowers after the service. Family often wants the baskets. Sprays are harder to reuse. Some families ask the shop to skip the most perishable blooms if the service is two days away.
  5. Pay the shop, not a vague "flower charge" added to the funeral contract, unless you have seen the shop's receipt.

Lower-cost choices that are still considerate

A single family spray plus a donation in place of other flowers is a complete choice. A potted plant costs less than a cut arrangement and can go home. Grocery-store bunches, arranged in a basket you already own, are appropriate from close friends when the family has said to keep it simple. None of these is a slight if the obituary invited simplicity.

For a child or an infant, ask the family before you send anything bright or large. Our infant and child funeral guide is the page for that situation. The flower order is not the place to guess.

After the service, and the bill that is not on the price list

Flowers are not a line in the NFDA 2023 General Price List Study. That study's $8,300 median for a viewing and burial leaves florists out, which is why this flower guide will not invent a national arrangement price. The bill you can audit is the shop's invoice and the funeral home's statement. If both charge for the same spray, you are paying twice, and the second charge is the one to question before you sign.

Cremation changes the order, not the manners. A family that chose direct cremation may still want flowers at a memorial a week later, in a church, a park, or a living room. Tell the shop the venue and whether there is a casket. A casket spray sent to a memorial with an urn is the wrong object. A basket or a standing piece fits. If the family asked for donations to a named charity, the card can name the charity and the shop does not need to deliver anything.

Keep the shop's written description, the delivery time, and the card copy. Those three lines are what you compare when a second relative offers to handle the flowers. Frequently Asked Questions

Can the funeral home mark up flowers?

Yes, but under the FTC Funeral Rule a funeral home that adds a fee to cash advance items such as flowers must disclose that in writing and must tell you about supplier rebates or discounts.

Who pays for the casket spray?

Usually the immediate family. Friends and organizations more often send standing sprays, wreaths, or plants.

Is it acceptable to ask for donations instead of flowers?

Yes. An "in lieu of flowers" line in the obituary naming a charity is common and widely understood.

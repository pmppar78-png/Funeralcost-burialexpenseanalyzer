Estate Planning Costs: Wills, Trusts and Funeral Pre-Planning

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Last updated: October 24, 2026 Last reviewed: October 22, 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, Government Sources Editorial: Our Standards

Estate planning is mostly about paperwork that saves your family time, money, and guesswork later: who gets what, who decides for you if you can't, and how your funeral will be paid for. Most families will never owe federal estate tax, so the useful questions are practical ones. This guide sorts the documents by what they do and what they cost you to skip.

First, the federal estate tax probably doesn't apply

For people who die in 2026, the federal basic exclusion amount is $15 million per person, and the annual gift exclusion is $19,000 per recipient (IRS: What's new, estate and gift tax). Some states have their own estate or inheritance taxes with much lower thresholds, so check your state's revenue department.

Paying for the plan

Attorney pricing varies widely by state and by complexity, and we couldn't find a reliable national survey, so we don't quote a range. Ask for flat-fee quotes for a defined document package and compare. Legal aid organizations and some employers' legal-plan benefits offer low-cost wills, and the American Bar Association's Find Legal Help page lists state resources.

The funeral part of the plan

Writing down your wishes is free and often the most valuable step. If you prepay, read our prepaid funeral plan guide first. Whatever you choose, the FTC Funeral Rule lets your family buy only the items they want from the funeral home's itemized price list (FTC: Funeral Costs and Pricing Checklist).

A weekend plan that does not require a fee quote we cannot source

The estate-planning cost guide already explains why most families will not owe federal estate tax in 2026, and it declines to invent a national attorney fee. This section is the practical sequence that sits next to that caution. Do it while everyone can still sign. None of these steps asks you to prepay a funeral.

Saturday morning: the one-page inventory

Write down every account that already has a beneficiary or a payable-on-death name: life insurance, IRAs, 401(k)s, and bank accounts. Those forms pass the money by contract. A later will does not rewrite them. If the form still names a former spouse or a parent who has died, fix the form at the institution, not in a letter kept at home. This is the highest-value hour in the estate-planning cost guide because it is free and it prevents the most common surprise.

Saturday afternoon: the four signatures

Most families need four documents, not a trust. A will names an executor and, if you have minor children, a guardian. A durable power of attorney names someone to handle money if you cannot. A health care proxy names someone to talk to doctors. An advance directive records treatment wishes. State forms for the health care papers are often free from the state health department or the bar. The American Bar Association's Find Legal Help page is the directory we use when someone asks where to start looking for a lawyer. Ask any lawyer for a flat fee for a defined package, in writing, before you compare. We still do not publish a national "average will price," because we do not have a survey that would support one.

Sunday: funeral wishes, separate from the will

Put burial or cremation wishes in a short letter your family can find, and tell them the letter exists. A will is often not read until after the funeral. For a funding target, use the medians from the NFDA 2023 General Price List Study (latest available): $8,300 for a funeral with viewing and burial, vault not included, and $6,280 for a funeral with viewing and cremation. Direct cremation medians in that same study, as reprinted in NFDA's 2024 Cremation and Burial Report, are $2,645 when the family supplies the container and $2,750 when the funeral home does. Those are national medians from member price lists surveyed in 2023, not a quote in your county.

If you want the money set aside without buying insurance, a payable-on-death savings account does that job. The person you name files a death certificate with the bank. That is not a funeral policy, and it does not lapse the way a policy can. Our funeral insurance guide explains policies. This estate-planning cost page is only about the documents and the account.

What the Funeral Rule changes about prepaying

The FTC Funeral Rule (16 CFR Part 453) requires itemized prices and lets the family decline items they do not want. It applies when a funeral provider sells arrangements, including many preneed sales. It does not make a prepaid contract a good idea. Read the cancellation, trust, and transfer terms before you pay. Our prepaid funeral plan guide is the place for that comparison. State trust rules differ; the state funeral board named in our regulations directory is the agency that usually writes them.

A note on state estate and inheritance taxes

With the federal exclusion at the level given above, most estates owe no federal estate tax, so for most families the costs on this page are probate, legal and funeral bills rather than tax. A minority of states impose their own estate or inheritance tax at much lower amounts. The list of those states changes. Check the revenue department where you live rather than relying on a blog list, including this one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will my estate owe federal estate tax in 2026?

Only if it exceeds the $15 million basic exclusion per person for deaths in 2026, per the IRS. Some states have lower estate or inheritance tax thresholds.

What estate planning documents matter most?

A will, current beneficiary designations, a durable power of attorney, and a health care proxy cover most families. A living trust is optional and mainly helps avoid probate.

Do beneficiary designations override a will?

Accounts with a named beneficiary generally pass directly to that person, regardless of the will, so keep the forms up to date.

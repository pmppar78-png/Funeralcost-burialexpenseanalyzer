Burial Costs in New York City, New York (2026)

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 State: New York

Burial in New York City is priced the way Manhattan real estate is priced — because, in a very real sense, that is what a cemetery plot here is. Plot scarcity in the five boroughs, the city's distinctive not-for-profit cemetery system, and the layered fees charged by historic grounds like Green-Wood in Brooklyn and Woodlawn in the Bronx push the full cost of a traditional burial far above the state average. Families planning a New York City burial in 2026 are generally looking at $27,378 to $38,610 once every line item is on the invoice.

Once every bill is on the table, a full New York City burial totals between $27,378 and $38,610. The spread reflects the gap between, say, a Catholic cemetery plot in Queens and a historic hillside space at Green-Wood — the numbers on the table are averages across that range, not a single quote.

Why the five boroughs have five different plot markets

Cemeteries are not interchangeable in New York. Green-Wood Cemetery in Brooklyn, founded in 1838, has been effectively sold out for decades; its remaining availability is small, non-standard, and priced accordingly. Woodlawn Cemetery in the Bronx still carries some traditional sections but has shifted significant inventory to mausoleums and cremation niches. Calvary Cemetery in Queens, owned by the Archdiocese of New York, is the largest active Catholic cemetery in the country by interments and prices its sections in tiers that can differ by several thousand dollars within the same grounds. Trinity Church Cemetery and Mausoleum, the only active cemetery in Manhattan, is essentially closed to new plot sales outside of its existing crypt spaces. The borough where burial happens, and the denomination of the cemetery, change the plot line more than any other factor.

Why New York has no for-profit cemeteries

New York State prohibits for-profit ownership of cemeteries in most cases. The New York Division of Cemeteries, part of the Department of State, regulates not-for-profit cemeteries, while the Bureau of Funeral Directing within the Department of Health oversees funeral homes themselves. The practical effect for a family: New York City cemeteries all operate on nonprofit charters with state-supervised pricing schedules, which is why the cemetery's price list may look more formal than what you see in other states. Rates change on a published schedule, and discounts are rare.

The Green-Wood waitlist reality and what families actually do about it

At Green-Wood, remaining in-ground inventory is limited to a handful of sections, and the cemetery has at times restricted new plot sales to existing lot-owning families. Mausoleum crypts at the Hill of Graves and the Tranquility Garden are where most new single and companion spaces are offered. Families unable to place a loved one at Green-Wood commonly pivot to Evergreens Cemetery, also in Brooklyn, to Cypress Hills on the Brooklyn-Queens border, or to one of the upstate nonprofit cemeteries where a drive of 30 to 60 minutes can cut the plot bill by half or more.

Funeral homes, transit permits, and five-borough logistics

Prestige funeral homes in Manhattan — Frank E. Campbell on the Upper East Side is the best-known example, with Riverside Memorial Chapel a longstanding alternative — operate at the high end of the service-fee scale and account for a meaningful share of the $14,040 average you see on the table. Outer-borough funeral homes in Queens, Brooklyn, and Staten Island frequently quote $3,000 to $6,000 less on the equivalent service package. If the burial is in New Jersey, Long Island outside the city, or upstate New York, a burial transit permit must be filed — this is handled by the funeral director but does add administrative time and, on occasion, an interstate-transport fee.

HRA burial assistance: the 120-day window matters

For families with limited resources, the New York City Human Resources Administration administers a one-time burial assistance payment of up to $1,700 for low-income residents. The application window is strict: claims must be filed within 120 days of the date of death, and the total funeral bill cannot exceed a cap ($3,400 in recent program rules, adjusted periodically). The program covers a direct burial or direct cremation path, not a full traditional service. For residents of Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens, Staten Island, and Manhattan, the HRA program is usually the first resource to check — and often needs to be paired with a funeral provider willing to work within the cap.

Places where New York City burial costs bend lower

  • Catholic, Jewish, and Orthodox cemeteries. Calvary in Queens, Mount Hebron in Flushing, and the various Eastern Orthodox sections across the metro frequently price members-of-the-faith plots $1,620 to $3,240 below the comparable space at a secular cemetery.
  • Two funeral homes, one cemetery. Price the cemetery and the funeral home separately, in writing. The cemetery invoice is never part of the General Price List the funeral home is required to share under the FTC Funeral Rule, and bundled quotes hide the larger markup.
  • Upstate or Long Island cemeteries. A 30-minute drive to Ferncliff in Westchester or a cemetery in Nassau can reduce the plot bill significantly. Out-of-borough visitation costs most families nothing extra.
  • Winter storage. New York cemeteries routinely stop opening graves when the ground is frozen. Cemeteries charge a winter vault fee to hold the remains until spring — ask what it is up front rather than discovering it in February.
  • Third-party caskets. Campbell, Riverside, and nearly every New York funeral home are bound by the FTC Funeral Rule and cannot refuse or surcharge a casket purchased online. Casket buying guide | Online retailers
Ask for the cemetery rules book: New York City cemeteries publish rules that govern monument styles, opening schedules, and winter storage. Green-Wood, Woodlawn, and Calvary all post current fee schedules on request. Get them in writing before you choose.

Questions From Families

How much does burial cost in New York City?

A full traditional burial in the five boroughs in 2026 runs about $14,040 for funeral-home service and $7,020 for a cemetery plot, plus $4,212 for a casket, $2,808 for a vault, $3,510 for opening and closing, and $1,500 to $3,500 for a marker. The total lands between $27,378 and $38,610. Prestige Manhattan firms push the service line toward the top of that range; outer-borough providers cluster near the bottom.

Is a burial vault required in New York City?

New York State does not require a vault. Essentially every active cemetery in the five boroughs does, because the grounds are old, heavily used, and engineered around standardized interment containers. Green-Wood, Woodlawn, Calvary, and Cypress Hills all have formal container requirements written into their rules. A basic concrete liner is typically acceptable and costs meaningfully less than a sealed bronze or stainless vault — ask for the cemetery's container specification sheet before buying.

What are the cheapest burial options in New York City?

The lowest-cost routes are denominational cemeteries within the family's tradition (Calvary for Catholic families, Mount Hebron and Beth El for Jewish families), a direct burial with no viewing and a minimum container, or a cemetery just outside the city limits — Nassau, Westchester, Bergen, or Hudson counties — where plot prices often drop by a third. The HRA burial assistance program can help low-income residents cover up to $1,700 if the full bill stays below its cap and the claim is filed within 120 days of death.

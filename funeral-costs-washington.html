Funeral Costs in Washington: What Families Pay in 2026

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, FTC Funeral Rule Region: West

Washington became the first state in the nation to legalize Natural Organic Reduction — human composting — in 2020, and the state followed up by permitting alkaline hydrolysis (flameless cremation). Those two decisions positioned Washington as the most disposition-diverse state in America, and Recompose, the Kent-based pioneer of NOR, remains the first dedicated human-composting facility to operate anywhere in the world. The broader Seattle market includes Columbia Funeral Home, Howden-Kennedy, Cremation Society of Washington, and historic Evergreen-Washelli. Per the NFDA 2024 GPL Study, Washington's $8,800 traditional average runs 12% above the national median, and the state's 76% cremation rate (CDC NCHS data) is among the highest in the country. The Washington Funeral and Cemetery Board has kept pace with one of the fastest-evolving regulatory frameworks in the industry, alongside the federal FTC Funeral Rule (16 CFR Part 453).

Washington's first-in-nation human composting law

Washington's 2020 statute authorizing Natural Organic Reduction — Senate Bill 5001 — made the state the first jurisdiction anywhere in the world to legalize human composting as a disposition alternative. Recompose operates the state's original facility in Kent, and additional NOR providers have since entered the Washington market. Current NOR pricing runs approximately $5,500 to $7,500 per disposition, placing it between cremation and traditional burial in cost. The process takes 30 to 60 days and produces a cubic yard of soil that can be returned to the family, scattered on dedicated conservation land, or donated to forest restoration projects. The WA Board of Funeral Directors and Embalmers regulates NOR providers alongside traditional funeral homes and crematories.

Alkaline hydrolysis as a cremation alternative

Washington legalized alkaline hydrolysis — sometimes called water cremation or flameless cremation — years before NOR. The process uses a heated alkaline solution to break down soft tissue, returning mineralized bone fragments similar to those from flame cremation. Pricing typically runs 10% to 20% above traditional flame cremation at providers that offer it, though the premium is narrowing as adoption grows. For families drawn to the lower environmental footprint, alkaline hydrolysis is a meaningful option; several Seattle and Spokane providers now offer it.

Tahoma National Cemetery and state burial benefits

Tahoma National Cemetery in Kent — the state's primary federal veteran cemetery — provides free burial for eligible Washington veterans and their dependents. The cemetery serves the bulk of Western Washington, while eastern Washington veterans sometimes route to the Medical Lake area or coordinate with Idaho's Boise National Cemetery. Washington's Department of Social and Health Services (DSHS) administers limited burial assistance for eligible low-income residents, typically covering direct cremation costs up to approximately $700, which is modest relative to costs but can be combined with other resources.

The east-west Cascades price divergence

Washington's Cascade Mountains divide the state into two distinct funeral economies. Puget Sound and the I-5 corridor — Seattle, Tacoma, Everett, Olympia — operate in a high-cost-of-living labor market where a traditional service can easily exceed $9,500. Spokane, Coeur d'Alene-adjacent eastern Washington, and the Columbia Basin run on substantially lower overhead, and comparable services often quote $7,500 or below. Families with flexibility on geography — and especially those where the decedent has ties on both sides of the mountains — sometimes save $2,000 or more by arranging through an eastern Washington provider for a body that will ultimately be buried or scattered west of the Cascades.

The line items that add up fast

Most of the pricing surprises in Washington come from a predictable list of add-ons. Look for these specifically when reviewing any funeral home quote:

  • Casket handling fee — Some funeral homes add a surcharge for caskets purchased elsewhere, despite this being illegal under the FTC Funeral Rule
  • Mandatory embalming — Funeral homes may claim embalming is required. In Washington, it is almost never legally required. Know your rights
  • Cemetery "perpetual care" fees — An ongoing maintenance charge, typically $200–$1,000, added on top of the plot price
  • Vault/liner surcharge — Cemeteries may require a specific vault brand or type, inflating costs by $500–$2,000+
  • Documentation and filing fees — Death certificates, permits, and filing fees can add $200–$600
  • Weekend/holiday premium — Services held outside business hours may carry a 15–25% surcharge
  • "Package" markups — Bundled packages often include services you don't need. Always compare line-by-line. What funeral homes don't tell you

What You're Entitled to Under Federal and State Rules

Know Your Rights Under the FTC Funeral Rule Every funeral home in Washington must comply with the Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule, which protects consumers nationwide. You have the right to: receive an itemized General Price List before making any decisions; choose only the services and products you want; purchase a casket or urn from a third party without penalty; decline embalming unless required by state law for specific circumstances; and receive a written estimate before services are performed.

If you believe a funeral home in Washington has violated these rights, you can file a complaint with the FTC at ftc.gov or contact the Funeral Consumers Alliance for guidance. Your state attorney general's office can also assist with consumer protection complaints.

Evergreen-Washelli, Lake View, and Seattle's historic cemetery layer

Evergreen-Washelli Memorial Park, founded in 1884, is one of Seattle's oldest and largest cemeteries and remains an active burial ground with both traditional and green-burial sections. Lake View Cemetery on Capitol Hill, founded in 1872 and the burial site of Bruce Lee and Brandon Lee, continues to sell plots as space permits. For families seeking a Seattle-specific historic setting, both cemeteries offer pricing that beats newer suburban memorial parks in Bellevue or Renton while providing substantially more mature landscaping and established neighborhood context.

Starting points for families

For anyone planning a funeral in Washington now or soon, these steps — in roughly this order — prevent the most common expensive mistakes:

  1. Take a breath. Unless there are legal or medical time constraints, you typically have 24–72 hours before decisions must be finalized.
  2. Request General Price Lists from at least 2–3 funeral homes in your area of Washington. They are legally required to provide them. Questions to ask funeral homes

Commonly Asked Questions

What is the average price of a funeral in Washington right now?

Current averages in Washington come in at $8,800 for a traditional funeral, $6,800 for cremation with a service, and from $2,100 for direct cremation — with the traditional figure sitting 12% above the $7,848 US median. Cemetery and burial fees add roughly $4,200 when applicable. Because Washington pricing is not standardized, the same service can swing several thousand dollars between neighboring funeral homes, so always ask for the itemized GPL.

Does cremation save money compared with burial in Washington?

It does. A direct cremation in Washington runs about $2,100, whereas a traditional funeral with burial averages $8,800 before adding the plot — so the gap can easily exceed $6,700. Washington's cremation rate of 76% sits above the roughly 60% national figure. Western states tend to have higher cremation rates, and the availability of green burial and alternative disposition options is growing across the region.

What are my consumer rights at Washington funeral homes?

The federal FTC Funeral Rule protects every consumer in Washington. Funeral homes must hand you an itemized General Price List on request, let you pick and choose services (packages cannot be forced on you), accept a casket or urn purchased elsewhere with no handling fee, and never misrepresent legal requirements. Washington may layer additional state protections on top through its funeral regulatory board.

How do I keep funeral costs down in Washington?

Three moves cut the most: compare at least three Washington General Price Lists side by side before choosing a provider, strip packages down to only the services you actually want, and choose direct cremation ($2,100) if cost is the priority. Washington has a mature green burial and direct cremation market; providers are accustomed to non-traditional arrangements and often price transparently. Conservation burial grounds and hybrid cemetery sections in Washington typically cost 30-50% less than traditional plots in the same cemetery. Then confirm eligibility for veteran, Medicaid, and Social Security survivor benefits.

How do funeral costs in Washington compare to other states?

Washington funerals are above the national average in most metro areas, though rural communities may see lower prices. The state's traditional-funeral average of $8,800 sits 12% above the $7,848 national median, and the West region overall trends toward higher pricing than the rest of the country.

Which funeral option costs the least in Washington?

Direct cremation carries the lowest price tag in Washington, from roughly $2,100. You skip the viewing, ceremony, and embalming — transport, cremation, and return of the ashes are all that are included. Direct burial is the next step up at about $5,280 and is still considerably cheaper than a traditional service.

Where do I look for low-cost funeral providers in Washington?

Start with GPL requests from at least three Washington funeral homes — that alone exposes the outliers. Reach out to the Funeral Consumers Alliance for Washington for member-run lists, shortlist dedicated direct cremation providers, ask every funeral home about their simplest no-frills package, and see if a nonprofit or cooperative provider serves your area of Washington.

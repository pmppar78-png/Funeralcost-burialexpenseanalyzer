Headstone and Monument Costs: Types, Materials and Prices

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Last updated: October 24, 2026 Last reviewed: October 22, 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, Government Sources Editorial: Our Standards

A headstone is often the last purchase a family makes, sometimes months after the funeral, when the bills from everything else have already arrived. It is also the purchase with the fewest consumer protections. This guide walks through the order of decisions, the rules that apply (and the ones that don't), and the questions that keep a marker from costing more than it should.

Start with the cemetery's rules, not the catalog

Before you price anything, get the cemetery's written rules for the section where the grave is. Many cemeteries limit marker size, material, and style (some sections allow only flat markers flush with the lawn), and some charge a setting or installation fee or require a foundation. Those rules can rule out half the options you see in a showroom, so ask for them in writing and ask what every fee will be, including any fee for a marker bought somewhere else.

Which protections apply

The FTC Funeral Rule gives you itemized prices and the right to buy only what you want, but it covers funeral providers. According to the Funeral Consumers Alliance, it does not apply to third-party sellers such as monument dealers, or to cemeteries that lack an on-site funeral home (Funeral Consumers Alliance: FTC Funeral Rule). In practice that means:

  • A monument dealer is not required to give you an itemized price list, so ask for one and get every quote in writing, including lettering, design work, delivery, installation, and tax.
  • If a funeral home sells you a marker as part of the arrangements, the Funeral Rule's itemized-statement requirements apply to that sale.
  • State consumer-protection law and your contract are your main tools with a dealer. Pay a deposit, not the full price, until the proof is approved.

Veterans: check this before you buy anything

The VA furnishes a government headstone or marker, or a medallion for a privately purchased stone, for eligible veterans at no charge, including in private cemeteries. The family usually still pays the private cemetery's setting fee. See our veteran burial benefits guide for the forms. VA also lists a headstone or marker allowance on its VA.gov: Veterans burial allowance page ($441 maximum for veterans who died on or after October 1, 2025).

A five-step buying sequence

  1. Rules: get the cemetery's size, material, and fee rules in writing.
  2. Benefits: confirm any VA or other benefit before ordering a private stone.
  3. Three quotes: include at least one independent dealer and one online seller, quoting the same size, material, and lettering.
  4. Proof: check spelling, dates, and emblems on a written proof before cutting. Mistakes after cutting are expensive.
  5. Delivery: confirm who arranges installation, who pays the cemetery's fee, and the timeline (ground settling can delay placement).

About prices

We don't publish a national price range for headstones because we couldn't find a current, methodical survey to support one. Price depends mostly on size, material (bronze, granite colors, marble), and lettering or artwork. The written quotes you collect in step 3 are the reliable numbers. If you share them with our cost assistant, it can help you compare like for like.

Headstones: what sets the price, without a fake national median

This headstone and monument guide does not publish a U.S. median price. The NFDA 2023 GPL study excluded monuments and cemetery charges from its $8,300 funeral median on purpose. Cemeteries are also outside most of the FTC Funeral Rule, which binds funeral providers. The useful facts are about materials, who is allowed to set the stone, and the one marker program that does publish a price of zero.

Materials you will actually be offered

Granite is the common upright stone because it holds a polish and a carved letter outdoors. Bronze is usually a plaque on a granite base, set flush or on a slant. Marble is softer and shows weather sooner, which is why many cemeteries no longer allow it. A flat grass marker sits flush with the lawn and can be mowed over. An upright monument needs a foundation, and the cemetery, not the dealer, usually pours that foundation and charges a setting fee. Ask for those three numbers separately: the stone, the foundation, and the setting fee. A package quote that blends them cannot be compared.

The cemetery's rule is the binding one

Cemeteries publish a list of allowed sizes, colors, and whether an outside dealer may deliver the stone. Some charge an inspection fee when the stone comes from elsewhere. That is not the funeral home's casket rule. The Funeral Rule's ban on casket-handling fees does not automatically give you the same right at the cemetery gate. Get the cemetery's marker rules in writing before you pay a dealer. If the cemetery requires its own bronze, an online granite price is irrelevant.

Timing

A stone is rarely ready for the funeral. Carving and shipping often take months. Jewish tradition delays the unveiling until months after burial, which happens to match how long a stone takes. Other families simply wait. You can bury first and order the marker when you can compare two dealers without a salesperson in the room. Put the cemetery's size limit on the order so you do not buy a monument the cemetery will reject.

The VA marker

Eligible veterans can receive a government headstone, marker, or medallion at no charge. Ask a private cemetery for its setting fee before you apply, because that charge still falls to the family. National cemeteries set the government marker without that private fee. The application and the eligibility rules are on our veteran burial benefits guide, which tracks the VA's own pages. Do not buy a retail stone until you know whether the VA will provide one.

Questions for the dealer and the cemetery

  1. What is the cemetery's maximum size, and is a flat marker required in this section?
  2. What is the setting fee, and who pours the foundation?
  3. May an outside dealer deliver, and is there an inspection fee?
  4. Does the quoted price include the vase, the portrait, and the second inscription later?
  5. How long from approval of the proof to installation?

Lettering mistakes are common and expensive to recut. Approve a written proof of the name and the dates before anyone cuts. That step costs nothing and it belongs on this headstone page because it saves more money than hunting for a national average we do not have.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the FTC Funeral Rule cover headstones?

Only when a funeral provider sells the marker. The Rule does not apply to independent monument dealers or to cemeteries without an on-site funeral home, so get written, itemized quotes from those sellers.

Can a cemetery make me buy the marker from them?

Ask for the cemetery's rules in writing, including any fee for markers bought elsewhere. State law varies; if a fee seems designed to block outside purchases, contact your state's cemetery regulator or attorney general.

Do veterans get a free headstone?

Eligible veterans can receive a government headstone, marker, or medallion at no charge from the VA, but a private cemetery may still charge to set it.

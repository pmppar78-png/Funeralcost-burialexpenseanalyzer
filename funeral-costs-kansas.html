Funeral Costs in Kansas: What Families Pay in 2026

By: Paul Paradis, Editor Updated: April 2026 Sources: NFDA, FCA, FTC Funeral Rule Region: Midwest

Kansas carries a $6,600 traditional-funeral average — $1,250 below the US median — a 50% cremation rate, and a statute (KSA 65-1768) requiring a 48-hour wait between death and cremation. The Penwell-Gabel chain dominates from Kansas City through Topeka into Wichita, while Flint Hills family operators still define pricing in the western two-thirds of the state. Penwell-Gabel: how one chain shapes three cities

Penwell-Gabel operates funeral and cremation facilities across Kansas City (KS), Topeka, and Wichita, making it the single largest funeral brand in the state. For consumers, this means that in all three metros there is a Penwell-Gabel price tier that sits at or slightly above the state average, and a shorter list of independents who consistently quote below it. Cozine Memorial Group in Wichita, founded in 1912, is the most prominent independent alternative in that market. Porter Funeral Homes operates multiple locations in the Kansas City metro area. Pulling GPLs from both the chain and at least one independent in your metro is the fastest way to see the spread.

KSA 65-1768 and the 48-hour cremation wait

Under Kansas Statutes Annotated 65-1768, cremation cannot take place less than 48 hours after the time of death, with narrow exceptions for communicable disease and court-ordered dispositions. In practice this means every Kansas direct cremation includes at least two days of refrigeration or embalming — funeral homes typically roll this into the flat-rate direct cremation price, but some itemize it separately. When comparing quotes, confirm whether "direct cremation" includes the mandatory holding period or adds it as a $100–$300 refrigeration line.

  • 48-hour wait: mandatory; cremation permit is not issued sooner.
  • Coroner sign-off: required in every Kansas county before cremation.
  • Authorization: surviving-spouse or next-of-kin written consent required.
  • Holding: refrigeration almost always acceptable; embalming not required.

KSA 15-1001 and Kansas's township-cemetery network

Kansas is one of a small group of states with an established township-cemetery system codified in statute. Under KSA 15-1001 and related sections, townships maintain public cemeteries funded by a small property-tax levy, and residents can be interred at little or no cost. The infrastructure is uneven — some township cemeteries are active, others are effectively closed — but for rural families this can mean a burial plot for a nominal fee rather than the $2,900 state average. Township trustees are listed in the county clerk's directory.

Fort Leavenworth, Flint Hills scattering, and the state's quieter options

Fort Leavenworth National Cemetery, in continuous use since 1862, is one of the oldest active national cemeteries west of the Mississippi and serves as the primary VA burial ground for eastern Kansas veterans. The Kansas Veterans Cemetery system operates four state-level cemeteries (WaKeeney, Fort Dodge, Winfield, Fort Riley) that provide similar benefits. For cremated remains, the Tallgrass Prairie National Preserve and broader Flint Hills region permit scattering with land-manager coordination, and some families time the ceremony to the prairie burn cycle. Leavenworth's federal penitentiary also maintains an internal indigent-burial program for inmates without claimed remains — an administrative footnote most Kansas families will never encounter, but it does explain why Leavenworth County's indigent disposition volume runs higher than its population would suggest.

Kansas-specific tip: If a cremation is on the table, confirm that the quoted price includes the KSA 65-1768 48-hour holding period. Also ask whether the provider is Penwell-Gabel or an independent — the pricing gap is real and worth a second phone call.

Your Consumer Rights in Kansas

Know Your Rights Under the FTC Funeral Rule Every funeral home in Kansas must comply with the Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule, which protects consumers nationwide. You have the right to: receive an itemized General Price List before making any decisions; choose only the services and products you want; purchase a casket or urn from a third party without penalty; decline embalming unless required by state law for specific circumstances; and receive a written estimate before services are performed.

If you believe a funeral home in Kansas has violated these rights, you can file a complaint with the FTC at ftc.gov, the Kansas State Board of Mortuary Arts, or the Funeral Consumers Alliance. The Kansas Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division also accepts complaints.

What to Do When Planning a Funeral in Kansas

If you are currently arranging a funeral in Kansas, here is a step-by-step approach that can save you time, stress, and money:

  1. Take a breath. Unless there are legal or medical time constraints, you typically have 24–72 hours before decisions must be finalized.
  2. Request General Price Lists from at least 2–3 funeral homes in your area of Kansas. They are legally required to provide them. Questions to ask funeral homes

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a typical Kansas funeral cost in 2026?

Most Kansas families paying for a full traditional service see the bill settle around $6,600 — 16% below the $7,848 national benchmark. Cremation with ceremony runs closer to $5,300, while a stripped-down direct cremation in Kansas can land near $1,500. Cemetery plot fees in Kansas add about $2,900. Actual prices shift a lot depending on which Kansas provider you call first, which is why comparing is non-negotiable.

How much cheaper is cremation than burial in Kansas?

Direct cremation in Kansas starts around $1,500; a traditional funeral with burial averages $6,600, not counting the cemetery plot or vault. That is a difference of at least $5,100 for most families. With Kansas's cremation rate at 50% (near the national rate of about 60%), cremation providers in the state are competitive. Midwestern communities often have strong local funeral home traditions, and independent providers may offer more competitive pricing than national chains.

What are my consumer rights at Kansas funeral homes?

The federal FTC Funeral Rule protects every consumer in Kansas. Funeral homes must hand you an itemized General Price List on request, let you pick and choose services (packages cannot be forced on you), accept a casket or urn purchased elsewhere with no handling fee, and never misrepresent legal requirements. Kansas may layer additional state protections on top through its funeral regulatory board.

What is the best way to reduce funeral expenses in Kansas?

The biggest lever is comparison shopping — two or three GPLs from different Kansas providers, compared line by line, consistently saves families $1,000 or more. Township and county cemeteries in Kansas rarely appear in online searches but are often half the price of private cemeteries — call the clerk's office in the county where burial will occur. Independent, family-owned funeral homes still dominate Kansas and tend to price more transparently than chain providers. At $1,500, direct cremation is the cheapest disposition option available in Kansas. Always check whether you qualify for veteran burial benefits, Medicaid funeral help, or the Social Security one-time death payment.

Do I have to embalm a loved one in Kansas?

Almost never. Kansas does not legally require embalming in typical circumstances; it is an option you can decline. Refrigeration works in its place for nearly all Kansas funeral timelines. Midwestern states generally follow standard embalming laws — it is not legally required in most circumstances, though providers may require it for public viewings. If a funeral home tells you embalming is legally required, ask them to cite the statute — the FTC Funeral Rule forbids that claim unless it is factually true.

How do funeral costs in Kansas compare to other states?

Kansas funerals are near or slightly below the national average, reflecting moderate costs of living across the region. The state's traditional-funeral average of $6,600 sits 16% below the $7,848 national median, and the Midwest region overall trends toward moderate to lower pricing than the rest of the country.

What is the least expensive way to handle a funeral in Kansas?

In Kansas, direct cremation at about $1,500 is the most affordable path — just transport, cremation, and the return of the remains. A memorial service can happen later, anywhere you choose. If burial is preferred, direct burial (no viewing or ceremony) runs near $3,960 and is the cheapest burial option.

What is the best way to find a cheap funeral home in Kansas?

Three steps: pull General Price Lists from multiple Kansas providers and compare the itemized charges; check with the Funeral Consumers Alliance Kansas chapter for nonprofit and consumer-friendly providers; and prioritize direct-cremation specialists or cooperative funeral homes where you can get the simplest service tier.
